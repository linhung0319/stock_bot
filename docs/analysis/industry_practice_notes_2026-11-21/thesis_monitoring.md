# Thesis monitoring after purchase: telling "thesis broken" from "price fell"

Research date: 2026-11-21. Context: long-only, multi-year holding periods.

Labels used on every finding:
- **[PRACTICE]** = a named firm or investor's documented process (usually self-described; not audited).
- **[EVIDENCE]** = empirical study with data.
- **[OPINION]** = practitioner view without data.

Verification level: "(read)" = I read the primary text in this session. "(search summary)" = taken from a search-result summary or secondary write-up only; check the primary before quoting numbers.

## 1. Documented processes for thesis tracking (written thesis, signposts, kill criteria, pre-mortems, devil's advocate, checklists, journals)

### Takeaway
The documented pattern is: write the sell triggers at purchase time (a one-page pre-mortem), assign someone to argue the opposite, and use checklists built from past mistakes. Almost all of these tools are applied before buying; named practitioners themselves admit they have no "in-flight" equivalent, which is exactly the gap the bot's periodic review is trying to fill.

### Cited Findings
- **[PRACTICE] (read)** Eric Burns (Sanford DeLand, SDL UK Buffettology) writes a company-specific one-page "pre-mortem" when each new investment is made, with a list of "sell triggers" covering under what circumstances the original thesis would be compromised. He says it "removes that element of emotion from the decision." Example: he sold Diageo after a strategy shift by the new CEO hit a trigger. — [AJ Bell, "Why a fund manager calls it quits on a stock" (dated 13 July 2026 per the page)](https://www.ajbell.co.uk/news/why-fund-manager-calls-it-quits-stock)
- **[PRACTICE] (read)** Jonathan Winton (Fidelity Special Values): before investing, the team sets a view of the business's earnings potential and the valuation multiple it will pay; the holding is reassessed if the external environment changes that view. He accepts "some risk you sell out too early and leave something on the table." — [AJ Bell](https://www.ajbell.co.uk/news/why-fund-manager-calls-it-quits-stock)
- **[PRACTICE] (read)** Baillie Gifford calls its approach "hold discipline" rather than "sell discipline". Stated sell reasons are only two: "We will sell if new information emerges which undermines our original investment thesis, or when we think that valuations indicate that the growth potential has already been fully recognised." It also states that almost every one of its most successful investments included periods "where the share price had fallen significantly, the near-term outlook appeared deeply uncertain", and that "the hardest, but ultimately most rewarding decision, is to do nothing." — [Baillie Gifford, "Our take on sell discipline" transcript, May 2024](https://media.bailliegifford.com/mws/ubvks4sa/20240524135041_baillie-gifford-film-our-take-on-sell-discipline-transcript-may-2024.pdf)
- **[PRACTICE] (search summary)** Baillie Gifford Japan Trust manager Matthew Brett (2022): "We're probably better at holding things than we are selling things"; he named how quickly the team can recognise a problem with the investment case as the key factor in selling. — [Trustnet](https://www.trustnet.com/news/13287370/fund/sectors)
- **[OPINION] (read)** Howard Marks, "Selling Out" (Oaktree memo, 13 Jan 2022): selling because the price is up, or because it is down, are both invalid reasons. Valid reasons: the "investment thesis seems less valid than it did previously" or the probability "that it will prove accurate" has declined, or another investment offers "a superior risk-adjusted prospective return". "Selling things because they're down is a mistake that can give the buyers great opportunities." Questions to ask before selling: what will replace it, what might be missed by switching, and whether cash would really do better. — [Oaktree, "Selling Out"](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/insights/memo/selling-out)
- **[PRACTICE] (search summary)** Pre-mortem origin: Gary Klein, "Performing a Project Premortem", Harvard Business Review, Sept 2007. The team assumes the project has already failed and generates reasons why. The often-quoted "30%" comes from Mitchell, Russo and Pennington (1989) on prospective hindsight, and it measured more reasons generated, not more accurate reasons. — [Klein article copy](https://comprobo24.github.io/assignments/performing_a_project_premortem.pdf); [Wikipedia: Premortem](https://www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/Premortem); [caution on the 30% figure](https://resources.rework.com/libraries/project-management/pre-mortem)
- **[PRACTICE] (read)** Alpha Theory (portfolio-construction software vendor, describing client best practice): assign a designated devil's advocate to each investment; have the analyst contact the most bearish sell-side analyst on a long idea; run the pre-mortem prompt "it is one year from now and the stock has lost half its value, what happened?"; and institutionalise the question "what are we missing?". Rationale given: analysts building a narrative become myopic and fold new information into the existing story or ignore it. — [Alpha Theory best practices, part two](https://www.alphatheory.com/blog/alpha-theory-best-practices-part-two)
- **[PRACTICE] (search summary)** Mawer Investment Management often assigns a research-team member to play devil's advocate and try to discredit a potential investment, and deliberately searches for sources holding a negative view of the company. — [Mawer via AdvisorAnalyst](https://advisoranalyst.com/?p=27434)
- **[PRACTICE] (search summary, firm unnamed in the result)** One firm requires analysts to write a devil's-advocate memo on one of their large holdings twice a year; the holding is debated openly and voted on by the three most senior investors. — [search result set including Alpha Theory best practices part three](https://www.alphatheory.com/blog/alpha-theory-best-practices-three)
- **[PRACTICE] (search summary)** A CFA Institute Research Foundation report on investment committees has a section on encouraging dissent and devil's advocacy as an incremental fix for committee decisions. — [CFA Institute RF, Scherer, investment committees](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/sites/default/files/docs/research-reports/rf_scherer_investmentcommittees_online.pdf)
- **[PRACTICE] (search summary)** Mohnish Pabrai and Guy Spier (profiled in Atul Gawande's *The Checklist Manifesto*): Pabrai runs the checklist after research and before buying; each new mistake becomes a new checklist item; he asks of each loss whether the cause was visible at the outset and says in most cases it was obvious. Spier: no investment passes every item; the list's job is to throw up issues to focus on. Spier also stated that both he and Pabrai use a pre-investment checklist but **no in-flight checklist**. Gawande: a good checklist is short; long lists are not taken seriously. — [MOI Global: Spier, Pabrai, Shearn on checklists](https://moiglobal.com/guy-spier-mohnish-pabrai-michael-shearn-investment-checklists/); [Jason Zweig, "Make a list, check it twice"](https://jasonzweig.com/make-a-list-check-it-twice/); [Spier on checklists (PDF)](https://fs.blog/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/Guy-Spier-the-Use-of-Checklists.pdf)
- **[OPINION] (search summary)** Annie Duke (*Quit*, 2022): kill criteria are set in advance and combine a state and a date: "If by (date), I have/haven't (reached a particular state), I'll quit." For investing she says to decide the exit signals before entering, and calls "quit while you're ahead" bad advice for stocks. — [ThinkAdvisor interview](https://thinkadvisor.com/2022/11/01/the-key-to-knowing-when-its-time-to-quit); [Wharton Nano Tool](https://executiveeducation.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/NanoTool-2023-05.pdf)
- **[PRACTICE] (search summary)** Bridgewater's "issue log": everyone must record anything that goes wrong, with severity and the responsible party, so root causes get diagnosed. Critical accounts say it became a blame tool with a minimum quota of logged issues per person. Sources conflict on how it works in practice. — [Farnam Street on Dalio](https://fs.blog/2011/09/management-lessons-from-ray-dalio/)

### Inferences
- The bot's "premise + breaks-if + user-approved exit conditions" structure matches the best-documented practice (Burns's one-page pre-mortem with sell triggers; Duke's kill criteria). What it lacks relative to Duke is the **date** half: a premise that says "X will happen" needs a "by when", otherwise "not yet" can never fail. This directly addresses weakness (b).
- Pabrai's "every mistake becomes a checklist item" is a cheap, documented way to grow a risk list that is **not** derived from the stock's own thesis: a standing cross-stock checklist run at each review addresses weakness (a).
- Spier's admission that there is no in-flight checklist means the industry itself has thin documented practice for post-purchase monitoring; the bot should not expect to find a ready-made standard.
- The pre-mortem evidence supports "generates more failure reasons", not "more accurate". Use it to widen the risk list, not as a verdict.

### Gaps
- Capital Group, T. Rowe Price, Sequoia Fund: I found no primary document in this session describing their thesis-tracking or sell process. Not searched deeply; treat as unknown rather than absent.
- Fundsmith Owner's Manual: the PDF returned HTTP 403, so its sell criteria are unverified here. [URL for a later manual check](https://www.fundsmith.co.uk/media/mv3abv1h/owners-manual.pdf).
- Decision journals: no data-backed source found on their effect on sell decisions.
- No source found linking Klein or Mauboussin directly to Pabrai's checklist.

## 2. Handling "unknown unknowns" (fresh-eyes re-underwriting, zero-based reviews, analyst rotation, independent review)

### Takeaway
Documented answers are organisational, not analytical: someone other than the thesis author is made responsible for the opposing case, on a schedule, and negative sources are searched for on purpose. I found the "would we buy it today?" question only as general advice, not as a documented firm procedure with a set frequency.

### Cited Findings
- **[PRACTICE] (read)** The standing question "what are we missing?" and a per-position devil's advocate (not the covering analyst) are the recommended mechanism for risks outside the written thesis. — [Alpha Theory](https://www.alphatheory.com/blog/alpha-theory-best-practices-part-two)
- **[PRACTICE] (search summary)** Twice-yearly devil's-advocate memo on a large holding, written by the analyst, debated openly, voted on by three senior investors (firm not named in the result). — [Alpha Theory best practices part three](https://www.alphatheory.com/blog/alpha-theory-best-practices-three)
- **[PRACTICE] (search summary)** Mawer: seeks out sources with a negative view (sell-side bears, competitors) rather than waiting for contrary evidence to arrive. — [Mawer via AdvisorAnalyst](https://advisoranalyst.com/?p=27434)
- **[PRACTICE] (search summary)** Vontobel pairs fundamental analysts with separate investigative analysts (journalist background) whose "often-differing opinions" create debate; the portfolio manager makes the final call. — [Vontobel, "Through the prism"](https://am.vontobel.com/en/insights/through-the-prism-how-investigative-journalists-can-enhance-equity-research)
- **[EVIDENCE] (read)** In the 783-portfolio institutional dataset, sell decisions made on earnings-announcement days (when fresh, decision-relevant information about existing holdings is in front of the manager) outperformed sells on other days by more than 150 basis points a year, and on those days sells beat the random-sell counterfactual. The authors conclude managers "do not lack the fundamental skill to sell well ... it is just not transferred." — [Akepanidtaworn, Di Mascio, Imas, Schmidt, NBER WP 29076](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29076/w29076.pdf)
- **[OPINION] (search summary)** "Would you buy it today if you were looking at it fresh?" appears as advice for reviewing an underperforming fund. — [Morningstar, "When to give up on an underperforming fund"](https://www.morningstar.com.au/funds/when-to-give-up-on-an-underperforming-fund)
- **[OPINION] (search summary)** CFA Institute blog argues analysts anchored on their own thesis carry that stubbornness forward when they become portfolio managers. — [CFA Institute Enterprising Investor, 2013](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/blogs/enterprising-investor/2013/stubborn-analysts-may-become-stubborn-portfolio-managers)

### Inferences
- For weakness (a) (review only checks written premises): the documented fix is a **separate role with a different brief**: not "check premise N" but "argue the stock is a sell; what is missing from the thesis?", fed by a deliberate search for bearish sources. In the bot this means a zero-based pass that does not receive the premise list first, run before the premise check, whose output is candidate new premises/risks for the user to accept or reject.
- For weakness (c) (drafter and checker share documents): the industry analogue is using a different person and different sources (Mawer's negative-source search, Vontobel's investigative analysts). The equivalent for an AI pipeline is a different evidence set (new filings and third-party material dated after the draft, plus an explicit bear-source search), not just a different prompt over the same documents.
- The earnings-day result is the strongest evidence that **when** the review happens matters: reviews tied to the arrival of new company information produce better sell decisions than reviews triggered by price or by a need for cash.

### Gaps
- No documented case found of scheduled zero-based re-underwriting with a stated frequency, of rotating the covering analyst, or of an independent risk team reviewing single-stock theses at a long-only firm. These practices are widely talked about; I could not source them to a named firm.
- No empirical study found measuring whether devil's-advocate or fresh-eyes reviews improve returns.

## 3. Mandatory review triggers on price (thresholds; forced sale or forced decision)

### Takeaway
Where a price trigger is documented at long-only firms, it is about 20% (absolute since purchase, or relative to benchmark over 12 months), and it forces a **decision** (re-examine, then add or sell), not a sale. No documented long-only fundamental process found here uses an automatic price-based sale.

### Cited Findings
- **[PRACTICE] (read)** Mick Dillon (Brown Advisory Global Leaders): a "drawdown review" is triggered when a share "has fallen 20% since he bought it" or has underperformed the fund's benchmark by 20% over 12 months. Purpose: decide whether it is "either a bargain opportunity or was a wrong decision." Outcome: "Following the review, we decide to either buy more or sell." "Having this review changes the mindset of the decision-making from defensive to offensive." — [interactive investor, 30 Dec 2022](https://ii.co.uk/analysis-commentary/falling-funds-should-investors-cut-their-losses-do-nothing-or-buy-more-ii526439)
- **[PRACTICE] (read)** Liontrust Global Innovation team: a "man overboard" process when a share is down more than 20%, which "triggers an evaluation of the business." James Dowey: "We do a deeper dive to assess the company. If our view on the company is unchanged and the reason for the share price fall is macro related, then we top up." — [interactive investor, 30 Dec 2022](https://ii.co.uk/analysis-commentary/falling-funds-should-investors-cut-their-losses-do-nothing-or-buy-more-ii526439)
- **[EVIDENCE] (search summary)** Mauboussin (Credit Suisse, 2015), "Managing the Man Overboard Moment": base rates from about 5,400 cases of a stock falling 10% or more in one day relative to the market, 1990–2014. The checklist splits cases by whether the drop was on an earnings announcement, and by prior momentum, valuation and business quality, then reads the historical 30/60/90-day excess returns for that category to suggest buy, hold or sell. The author frames it as a guard against emotional decisions, not a final answer. — [ValueWalk summary](https://www.valuewalk.com/mauboussins-checklist-reacting-sudden-price-drops/); [Stockopedia summary](https://stockopedia.com/content/what-should-you-do-when-a-stock-plummets-buy-hold-or-sell-91386); [The 7 Circles summary](https://the7circles.uk/?p=27686)
- **[OPINION] (search summary)** John Dorfman (Dorfman Value Investments): a decline of 20% or more triggers a hard look; 30% creates a rebuttable presumption of sale, which he says should be relaxed in a market-wide selloff such as 2008. — [TribLive column](https://triblive.com/business/john-dorfman-what-is-your-sell-discipline-do-you-have-one)
- **[PRACTICE] (read)** Baillie Gifford explicitly rejects price falls as a sell signal (see section 1). — [Baillie Gifford transcript](https://media.bailliegifford.com/mws/ubvks4sa/20240524135041_baillie-gifford-film-our-take-on-sell-discipline-transcript-may-2024.pdf)
- **[EVIDENCE] (search summary)** Man Institute, on drawdown rules for firing managers: a 20% drawdown is common even for managers with respectable risk-adjusted returns, and a known drawdown rule changes the behaviour of the person subject to it. — [Man Institute, "You're fired"](https://www.man.com/maninstitute/youre-fired)
- **[PRACTICE] (read)** Bill Ackman sold Netflix about three months after buying, with no formal threshold, saying he had "lost confidence in our ability to predict the company's future prospects with a sufficient degree of certainty." — [interactive investor](https://ii.co.uk/analysis-commentary/falling-funds-should-investors-cut-their-losses-do-nothing-or-buy-more-ii526439)

### Inferences
- The bot's rule (halve at 20% below the 52-week high) uses the same number the two documented long-only processes use, but with the opposite consequence: they force a review whose output is "add or sell"; the bot forces a sale of half. Its rule is closer to a trend/stop rule (section 4) than to institutional fundamental practice. Both can coexist, but they answer different questions: the mechanical halving is risk control that needs no judgement; the drawdown review is the thesis check. The industry pattern suggests the 20% event should **also** open a mandatory thesis review with a required written outcome.
- Two refinements appear in the documented processes that the bot's trigger lacks: (1) a **relative** trigger (20% behind benchmark over 12 months), which separates "this stock fell" from "everything fell"; Liontrust's rule tops up specifically when the cause is macro and the company view is unchanged; (2) the review must end in a decision, never "no change by default".
- Mauboussin's split by "earnings-related or not" fits the same idea: a fall on company news deserves a different default from a fall with the market.

### Gaps
- No prospectus or stewardship document found that states a price-triggered review threshold; both documented cases come from manager interviews in one article.
- I could not reach Mauboussin's original PDF, so the exact base-rate tables are unverified.
- No evidence found on whether review-on-drawdown processes improve outcomes.

## 4. Evidence on stop-loss and trend rules for long-term investors, and re-entry

### Takeaway
Stop and trend rules are supported as **risk reduction** (smaller drawdowns, lower volatility), mainly at index/asset-class level and where returns show momentum; they are not shown to raise returns for individual fundamentally chosen stocks, and under a random walk they lower expected return. Evidence on re-entry rules is thin.

### Cited Findings
- **[EVIDENCE] (search summary of abstract)** Kaminski and Lo, "When Do Stop-Loss Rules Stop Losses?" (Journal of Financial Markets, 2014): if returns follow a random walk, a stop-loss rule always reduces expected return (the "stopping premium" is negative). It can add value when returns have momentum or regime switching. Empirically, on daily futures data Jan 1993–Nov 2011, stop-loss rules appear to exploit conditional momentum after losses in equities. The test is on expected return and separately notes volatility reduction. — [MIT DSpace record](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/114876); [full text PDF](https://dspace.mit.edu/bitstream/handle/1721.1/114876/Lo_When%20Do%20Stop-Loss.pdf)
- **[EVIDENCE] (search summary of abstract)** Han, Zhou and Zhu, "Taming Momentum Crashes: A Simple Stop-Loss Strategy": applied to a momentum **long-short stock portfolio**, 1926–2013, a 10% stop cut the worst monthly loss from −49.79% to −11.36% (equal-weighted) and roughly doubled the Sharpe ratio (0.369 vs 0.165). Results are gross of trading costs; reviewers note the extra trading reduces net performance. — [CXO Advisory review](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/technical-trading/stop-losses-to-avoid-stock-momentum-crashes/); [Alpha Architect review](https://alphaarchitect.com/2016/08/taming-the-momentum-roller-coaster-fact-or-fiction/)
- **[EVIDENCE] (search summary)** Daniel and Moskowitz, "Momentum Crashes" (Journal of Financial Economics, 2016): momentum strategies crash in "panic" states, after market declines and when volatility is high, and the crashes are "contemporaneous with market rebounds". Past losers behave like options and rebound sharply. — [NBER WP 20439](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20439)
- **[EVIDENCE] (search summary, secondary)** Faber, "A Quantitative Approach to Tactical Asset Allocation" (10-month moving average on asset classes): a replication for 1973–2011 reports maximum drawdown of −9.56% with timing against −46.00% buy-and-hold, and Sharpe 0.73 against 0.39, for the multi-asset portfolio. A later revision of the paper notes drawdowns became larger once 2008–2012 data were added. An independent test found little return benefit over a diversified baseline. — [Juniata replication](https://admissions.juniata.edu/offices/juniata-voices/media/andrew-and-castro.pdf); [ThinkAdvisor on the paper](https://thinkadvisor.com/2015/09/02/most-downloaded-ssrn-paper-ever-says-you-should-be/?amp=1); [Butler/Philbrick critique](https://m.investing.com/analysis/permanent-portfolio-shakedown,-part-ii-134270?ampMode=1)
- **[EVIDENCE] (read)** Institutional managers who become more likely to sell positions with extreme past returns (top quartile of that tendency) give up almost 180 basis points a year on those sales; and managers "rarely re-purchase assets that have left their portfolio", so a sale tends to be permanent. Momentum-style managers show the least sell underperformance. — [NBER WP 29076](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29076/w29076.pdf)

### Inferences
- The supporting evidence is for diversified portfolios, futures and long-short momentum books. None of the papers found tests "halve a single fundamentally chosen stock at −20% from its high". The bot's rule should be justified as drawdown control, not as a return source, and the write-up should say so.
- Daniel–Moskowitz implies the main cost of such a rule: after a market-wide fall with high volatility, beaten-down stocks rebound fastest, which is when a 200-day re-entry rule is still out. A partial sale (half, as the bot does) limits this cost; it is a reasonable hedge between the Kaminski–Lo "random walk" case and the "momentum" case.
- The NBER finding that sold names are rarely bought back is an argument for keeping an explicit mechanical re-entry rule at all: without one, a halved position tends to stay halved.
- A stop measured from the 52-week high conflates stock-specific and market-wide falls. The literature above suggests the two cases have different expected follow-through (company news versus panic rebound), which supports recording the market's own drawdown alongside the trigger.

### Gaps
- No study found on re-entry rules specifically (for example N consecutive closes above the 200-day average) for individual stocks. The choice of 10 closes is not evidence-based as far as this search shows.
- I did not read the Kaminski–Lo, Han–Zhou–Zhu or Faber full texts in this session; the numbers above come from abstracts and secondary reviews.
- No source found testing drawdown-from-high stops on single stocks held for fundamental reasons over multi-year horizons.

## 5. Evidence on selling behaviour and what improves sell decisions

### Takeaway
Professional managers buy with skill and sell worse than random, because selling gets little attention and is steered by extreme past returns (both big winners and big losers). The remedies supported by data are: tie sell reviews to fresh information, review the whole portfolio rather than only the extremes, measure sells against a counterfactual, and use decision aids or simple rules.

### Cited Findings
- **[EVIDENCE] (read)** Akepanidtaworn, Di Mascio, Imas, Schmidt, "Selling Fast and Buying Slow" (NBER WP 29076, July 2021; later Journal of Finance 2023). Data: 783 institutional portfolios averaging about $573 million, 2000–2016, 4.4 million trades (2.0 million sells, 2.4 million buys). Buys beat a random-buy counterfactual by over 100 basis points a year; sells underperform a random-sell counterfactual, and "in our preferred specification, PMs forgo 80 basis points per year relative to a factor neutral, random-selling strategy." — [NBER WP 29076](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29076/w29076.pdf)
- **[EVIDENCE] (read)** Mechanism: a two-stage heuristic. Limited attention narrows the candidates to positions with extreme past returns; "both the worst and best performing assets in the portfolio are sold at rates more than 50 percent higher than assets that just under- or over-performed." From that set managers sell the positions they have least conviction in ("neglected ideas", low active weight), and those sales account for much of the underperformance; sales of high-conviction positions show no systematic underperformance. Buying shows no such link to past returns. — [NBER WP 29076](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29076/w29076.pdf)
- **[EVIDENCE] (read)** Underperformance is worst among fundamentals-oriented managers with concentrated, high-tracking-error portfolios; it worsens when the whole portfolio is underwater (stress) and when selling is done to raise cash for a purchase. Manager quotes: "Selling is simply a cash raising exercise for the next buying idea." — [NBER WP 29076](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29076/w29076.pdf)
- **[EVIDENCE] (read)** Authors' prescriptions: reporting "that emphasize[s] counterfactual sales performance" so learning can occur, and "adoption of decision aids and/or simple alternative selling strategies may substantially improve performance." They note managers may never notice the problem because feedback on sells is weak. — [NBER WP 29076](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29076/w29076.pdf)
- **[EVIDENCE] (search summary; vendor research)** Essentia Analytics, "The Alpha Life Cycle" (Journal of Investing, 2022): 9,254 position episodes across 43 active equity portfolios, 2005–2019; alpha "starts out strong and fades sharply with age"; authors point to the endowment effect. A 2020 Essentia study of 60 portfolios found most value was destroyed by holding losers too long and exiting into falling prices, and estimated earlier exits could have saved an average of 94 basis points a year. Essentia sells behavioural analytics, and the average holding period in the sample was under a year, so this is not a multi-year-holder sample. — [Institutional Investor](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/b1hxzwxndzj7rc/Active-Equity-Managers-Actually-Do-Generate-Alpha-Here-s-How-They-Squander-It); [Alpha Architect review](https://alphaarchitect.com/active-managers-bias/)
- **[EVIDENCE] (search summary; dated 2007)** Inalytics study of about 45,000 institutional trades: managers sold 57% of stocks that went on to outperform and kept 43% of underperformers; performance typically turned negative in the month before the sale, read as short-term price weakness being used in place of research. — [Investment Magazine, 2007](https://www.investmentmagazine.com.au/2007/04/managers-better-at-buying-than-selling-stocks-inalytics-report/)
- **[PRACTICE] (search summary)** Cabot (now part of FactSet) scores managers separately on Buy Skill, Sell Skill (effectiveness of final sells) and Sizing Skill, i.e. sell decisions are measured as their own skill. — [FactSet Insight](https://insight.factset.com/quantifying-portfolio-managers-skills-for-a-more-vibrant-active-equities-industry)

### Inferences
- The two evidence strands point in opposite directions and the bot needs both: Essentia/Inalytics say managers hold deteriorating positions too long; NBER says managers sell on price extremes and regret it. The reconciliation is that the **trigger** for a sell should be thesis evidence, not the price move, and the price move should only trigger attention. This is the same conclusion as section 3.
- Direct design consequences: (1) run the thesis review on a calendar tied to earnings releases for every holding, not only for holdings that moved a lot; (2) keep a sell ledger that records, for each sale or halving, what the stock did afterwards against what the rest of the portfolio did (the counterfactual report the authors recommend); (3) treat low-weight, rarely reviewed holdings as the highest-risk group for a bad sale.
- The disposition effect itself (reluctance to realise losses) was not separately sourced in this session; the NBER paper cites Ben-David and Hirshleifer (2012) and Odean's work as the standard references.

### Gaps
- No controlled evidence found that a specific process (pre-mortem, checklist, kill criteria) improves realised sell performance. The NBER authors only suggest it.
- Journal of Finance published version not read; figures are from the 2021 working paper.
- Essentia and Inalytics figures are from secondary coverage of vendor research and should be labelled as such.

## 6. Evidence freshness and evidence quality in thesis reviews

### Takeaway
I found no documented buy-side standard for grading evidence as confirmed, unconfirmed or stale. What is documented is indirect: date-bound kill criteria, the superior quality of decisions made when fresh company information arrives, and data showing analyst estimates go out of date within about a quarter.

### Cited Findings
- **[EVIDENCE] (read)** Sell decisions improve by more than 150 basis points a year when made on days decision-relevant information about the holding is released (earnings days). — [NBER WP 29076](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29076/w29076.pdf)
- **[EVIDENCE] (search summary)** A Columbia paper on sell-side analyst ideas reports that 50% of target-price forecasts are revised within 90 days, i.e. the half-life of an analyst's published view is about one quarter. — [Columbia Business School seminar paper, Livnat, Feb 2025](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/imce-uploads/ADP/Spring%202025/Joshua%20Livnat%20Feb%202025.pdf)
- **[EVIDENCE] (search summary; vendor study)** Visible Alpha: analyst and consensus revisions flow continuously and "cannot be summarized by stale snapshots"; roughly half of revisions have already happened by the time a periodic update is received. — [Visible Alpha analyst revisions study](https://irsociety.org.uk/files/Analyst_Revisions_Study_IR_Edition_060420_-_Visible_Alpha.pdf)
- **[OPINION] (search summary)** Duke's "state and date" form makes the absence of confirming evidence by a deadline count as a failure signal rather than as "still holding". — [Wharton Nano Tool](https://executiveeducation.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/NanoTool-2023-05.pdf)
- **[PRACTICE] (search summary)** Deliberately seeking sources with a negative view (Mawer), contacting the most bearish analyst (Alpha Theory), and using separate investigative analysts (Vontobel) are the documented ways to get evidence that does not originate from the company or from the thesis author. — [Mawer](https://advisoranalyst.com/?p=27434); [Alpha Theory](https://www.alphatheory.com/blog/alpha-theory-best-practices-part-two); [Vontobel](https://am.vontobel.com/en/insights/through-the-prism-how-investigative-journalists-can-enhance-equity-research)
- **[PRACTICE] (read)** Baillie Gifford's stated sell test is "new information ... which undermines our original investment thesis": the test is defined on **new** information, which presupposes knowing what is new since the last review. — [Baillie Gifford transcript](https://media.bailliegifford.com/mws/ubvks4sa/20240524135041_baillie-gifford-film-our-take-on-sell-discipline-transcript-may-2024.pdf)

### Inferences
- For weakness (b): the review verdict needs more than two states. A minimum set consistent with the sources is: **confirmed by evidence dated after the last review**; **contradicted**; **unverified this period** (no new evidence either way; the last confirmation date is shown); **overdue** (the premise had a date and it has passed). "No contrary evidence" should map to "unverified this period", never to "holds". A premise unverified for more than a set number of reviews, or about one to two reporting quarters given the roughly 90-day half-life of analyst views, should escalate.
- Each premise should carry its expected evidence source and cadence at drafting time (for example "segment revenue, quarterly filing" or "regulator decision, by a date"), so that staleness is measurable by the program instead of judged by the AI.
- For weakness (d) (premises only third parties can verify): the documented practice is to go and get the outside view on purpose and to name it. The bot equivalent is to tag such premises as "third-party-only", require the source to be named and dated, cap how long one third-party datum can keep a premise "confirmed", and prefer to rewrite the premise into something the company's own filings will reveal later (a lagging but verifiable proxy) alongside the leading third-party indicator.
- For weakness (c): requiring that confirming quotes come from documents dated after the thesis draft gives a mechanical independence check; a verdict that quotes only documents the drafter also used is a restatement, not a verification.

### Gaps
- No documented firm process found for classifying evidence as confirmed / unconfirmed / stale, and none for weighting leading indicators against reported financials in a thesis review. This is the weakest-sourced section; the inferences above are my own construction from adjacent evidence.
- Channel checks, expert networks and third-party industry data: no source found in this session describing how long-only firms validate or age such inputs.
- The CFA Institute "mosaic theory" material was not retrieved.

## 7. Position-size and concentration limits independent of conviction

### Takeaway
Regulated funds face hard issuer caps that apply whatever the manager believes (US: 5% per issuer across 75% of assets; UCITS: 10% per issuer, and positions above 5% may not total more than 40%). Some managers add their own pre-set maximum per stock, fixed at purchase, and trim when it is hit.

### Cited Findings
- **[PRACTICE / regulation] (search summary of statute)** US Investment Company Act of 1940, section 5(b)(1): to call itself "diversified", a fund must have at least 75% of total assets invested so that no single issuer exceeds 5% of total assets and the fund holds no more than 10% of that issuer's voting securities. The remaining 25% is unconstrained by this test. — [15 U.S.C. § 80a-5, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/80a-5); [SEC staff report on threshold limits for diversified funds](https://SEC.gov/files/staff-report-threshold-limits-diversified-funds.pdf)
- **[PRACTICE / regulation] (search summary, secondary)** UCITS "5/10/40": no more than 10% of net asset value in one issuer; holdings above 5% may not exceed 40% of net asset value in total. — [Wikipedia: UCITS](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/UCITS)
- **[PRACTICE] (search summary)** These caps can make funds forced sellers of a stock that has risen strongly, regardless of the manager's view. — [interactive investor, "How fund rules can make some pros forced sellers when a stock soars"](https://www.ii.co.uk/analysis-commentary/how-fund-rules-can-make-some-pros-forced-sellers-when-stock-soars-ii530092)
- **[PRACTICE] (read)** Evenlode Global Equity (Chris Elliott, Cristina Dyer): each stock is given a pre-determined maximum position; when a holding reaches the ceiling it will "flash red" and the team trims to stay under it. Elliott calls this "nudging the portfolio." — [AJ Bell](https://www.ajbell.co.uk/news/why-fund-manager-calls-it-quits-stock)
- **[EVIDENCE] (read)** In the institutional dataset, the positions sold badly are the low-weight "neglected ideas"; sales of high-conviction (high active weight) positions do not systematically underperform. — [NBER WP 29076](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29076/w29076.pdf)
- **[PRACTICE] (search summary)** Cabot/FactSet measures Sizing Skill separately from Buy and Sell Skill, treating position sizing as its own decision. — [FactSet Insight](https://insight.factset.com/quantifying-portfolio-managers-skills-for-a-more-vibrant-active-equities-industry)

### Inferences
- A per-stock ceiling set at purchase (Evenlode style) plus a portfolio-level cap on the total weight of large positions (UCITS 40% style) are both simple, documented, and independent of the thesis verdict. They bound the damage from a thesis the review wrongly keeps passing, which is the residual risk left by weaknesses (a) to (d).
- The NBER result suggests a second sizing rule: avoid a long tail of small positions that never get reviewed, because that is where bad sell decisions concentrate.

### Gaps
- UCITS limits are from a secondary source; the directive text was not checked.
- No source retrieved on internal risk-team limits (sector, factor, single-name) at specific long-only firms, or on Fundsmith / Sequoia concentration policies.
- No evidence found on what ceiling level is optimal for a concentrated personal portfolio.

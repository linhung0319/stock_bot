# Crash, regime and large-move detection for a long-only, long-horizon stock bot

Research date: 2026-11-21. Scope: daily data, long-only equities, no intraday or options techniques.
Reading guide: "Cited Findings" are only what a fetched page or search result showed in this session. Several results came from search-result summaries or abstracts rather than full-text reads; these are marked "(abstract/summary level)". Numbers I recall from the literature but could not confirm in this session are listed under "Gaps" and labelled "recalled, unverified" so the report writer can decide whether to re-check them.

## 1. Market-level crash/regime indicators: which are robust, which are fragile, typical thresholds

### Takeaway
The only market-level signals with long, repeatedly replicated evidence are slow price trend (200-day / 10-month average and neighbours) and volatility level; both reduce drawdowns far more reliably than they raise returns, and both lose much of their edge after costs and real-time estimation. Correlation/absorption-ratio, turbulence, VIX term structure, breadth thrusts, HMM regime models and credit spreads have thinner or contested out-of-sample support and are better used as "widen review" context than as trade triggers.

### Cited Findings

Trend filters (200-day, 10-month, Faber)
- Faber's 2007 paper used data through 2005; a later review adding 11 years of out-of-sample returns reported the Sharpe ratio of the timing approach improving from 0.45 to 0.80 (secondary write-up by Swedroe, not Faber's own paper) — [ETF.com / Swedroe](https://www.etf.com/sections/index-investor-corner/swedroe-why-financial-trends-persist)
- A Journal of Asset Management study running out-of-sample tests of moving-average and time-series-momentum rules with realistic transaction costs concluded that the performance of market timing strategies is "highly overstated" (abstract/summary level) — [Journal of Asset Management (Springer)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/jam.2014.25)
- CXO Advisory review: data-snooping bias from selecting specifically the 10-month SMA may be material for US stocks, but timing beat buy-and-hold on gross Sharpe ratio for 3-, 6-, 9- and 12-month SMAs alike, i.e. the trend idea is more robust than the exact window — [CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/technical-trading/long-term-outperformance-from-trends-defined-by-moving-averages)
- A City University London (SSRN) paper finds a range of simple rules including the 200-day moving average rule dominate passive long-only index investment (abstract/summary level; sample and drawdown figures not verified) — [City Univ. open access](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/17842/8/BLACKBOX%20%20%20SSRN-id2126476.pdf)
- Whipsaw size, practitioner test: S&P 500 vs 200-day SMA, 1994-2019, 90 completed bullish signals, 20 winners averaging +13.69% and 70 losers averaging -1.32% (win rate about 22%; profits come from a few long trends) — [StockCharts, Arthur Hill 2019](https://stockcharts.com/articles/arthurhill/2019/02/just-how-effective-is-the-200-day-moving-average.html)
- Same author: a 5-day/200-day cross on IWM over about twenty years had only 41% winning signals; using a 5-day SMA of price instead of the raw close for signals lowers maximum drawdown and whipsaws — [StockCharts, Arthur Hill 2018](https://www.stockcharts.com/articles/arthurhill/2018/10/systemtrader---reducing-moving-average-whipsaws-with-smoothing-and-quantifying-filters-.html)
- Informal backtest: adding +/-5% bands around the 10-month average reduced whipsaw and turnover (blog source, low authority) — [freefincal](https://freefincal.com/?p=24910)

Volatility level and volatility-managed portfolios
- Moreira & Muir (Journal of Finance 72(4), 2017, pp. 1611-1644): scale monthly exposure by the inverse of the previous month's realized variance; "managed portfolios that take less risk when volatility is high produce large alphas, increase Sharpe ratios, and produce large utility gains for mean-variance investors"; documented for the market, value, momentum, profitability, ROE and investment factors and currency carry — [NBER w22208](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22208)
- Cederburg, O'Doherty, Wang & Yan (JFE 138(1), 2020): across 103 equity strategies, volatility-managed versions do not systematically beat unmanaged ones in direct comparisons; spanning-regression alphas are positive in-sample, but real-time (out-of-sample) versions usually earn lower certainty-equivalent returns and Sharpe ratios, attributed to unstable regression parameters — [Lehigh PDF](https://www.lehigh.edu/~xuy219/research/COWY.pdf); [Alpha Architect summary](https://alphaarchitect.com/does-portfolio-timing-based-on-volatility-signals-outperform-buy-and-hold/); [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:138:y:2020:i:1:p:95-117)
- Barroso & Detzel (2021) are cited as finding volatility-managed strategies do not survive transaction costs; DeMiguel, Martin-Utrera & Uppal (Journal of Finance 2024) find a conditional multifactor portfolio that scales factor weights with market volatility still beats the unconditional one out-of-sample net of costs, with a Sharpe ratio about 13% higher (a multifactor result, not a rebuttal for the single market factor) — [DeMiguel et al. PDF](https://lbsresearch.london.edu/id/eprint/3716/1/The%20Journal%20of%20Finance%20-%202024%20-%20DeMIGUEL%20-%20A%20Multifactor%20Perspective%20on%20Volatility%E2%80%90Managed%20Portfolios.pdf)

Absorption ratio (Kritzman, Li, Page, Rigobon; JPM Summer 2011)
- Definition: fraction of total variance of a set of asset returns explained ("absorbed") by a fixed number of eigenvectors; authors report most significant US stock drawdowns were preceded by spikes in the ratio, stock prices depreciated significantly after spikes, and the ratio rose ahead of market turbulence (abstract level) — [State Street Associates](https://globalmarkets.statestreet.com/research/portal/insights/article/2a8e282a-343b-443c-b362-1634c71d2d26)
- Signal form: the "standardized shift" subtracts a longer average from a 15-day moving average of the ratio (z-score-like); the original trading idea moved a 50% stocks / 50% government bonds portfolio's stock exposure (0% / 50% / 100%) on significant shifts in the MSCI USA absorption ratio; literature standard is to retain about 1/5 as many eigenvectors as assets — [Portfolio Optimizer blog](https://portfoliooptimizer.io/blog/the-absorption-ratio-measuring-financial-risk/); [Liverpool paper describing the 15-day step](https://www.liverpool.ac.uk/media/livacuk/schoolofmanagement/research/economics/Application,Absorption,Ratio.pdf)
- Independent re-check (9 sector SPDR ETFs, 52 weeks of weekly returns, Oct 2000 - Mar 2022): the ratio tended to DECREASE just before crashes (contradicting the original claim), spike during the crash and stay elevated afterwards; a scaled-exposure rule had average exposure about 50%, Sharpe about 0.85 vs 0.54 buy-and-hold and max weekly drawdown about 15% vs 55%, but this is one full-period backtest, not out-of-sample, and it struggled when the ratio was choppy (2005, 2015) — [Portfolio Optimizer blog](https://portfoliooptimizer.io/blog/the-absorption-ratio-measuring-financial-risk/)

Turbulence index (Kritzman & Li, FAJ 66(5), 2010)
- Turbulence = statistical unusualness of a cross-section of returns relative to history (Mahalanobis distance); "turbulence may arrive unexpectedly but does not immediately subside, typically continuing for weeks"; returns to risk are substantially lower in turbulent than non-turbulent periods; proposed uses are stress-testing with turbulent-subsample covariances and scaling exposure (abstract level) — [CFA Institute FAJ](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/2010/skulls-financial-turbulence-and-risk-management); [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:taf:ufajxx:v:66:y:2010:i:5:p:30-41)

VIX term structure
- Cboe (2022): VIX futures backwardation occurred less than 20% of the time since 2010; outside the largest, most memorable sell-offs, backwardation as a predictor of falling markets "doesn't pan out", and markets can recover quickly — [Cboe Insights](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/inside-volatility-trading-is-vix-backwardation-necessarily-a-sign-of-a-future-down-market)
- Fassas & Hourvouliades (JRFM 2019): VIX futures term structure works as a CONTRARIAN market-timing indicator (i.e. inversion tends to mark fear, not a sell signal) (abstract level) — [MDPI JRFM 12(3):113](https://mdpi.com/1911-8074/12/3/113)

Credit spreads
- Gilchrist & Zakrajsek split corporate spreads into expected default and a residual "excess bond premium" (EBP); a rise in EBP reflects reduced risk appetite of the financial sector and a contraction of credit supply — [NBER w17021](https://www.nber.org/papers/w17021.pdf)
- Fed FEDS Note (Favara, Gilchrist, Lewis, Zakrajsek 2016): probit of recession within 12 months; EBP marginal effect about 0.30-0.33, significant at 1%; the Fed publishes monthly EBP and the implied recession probability — [Federal Reserve FEDS Notes](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econresdata/notes/feds-notes/2016/recession-risk-and-the-excess-bond-premium-20160408.html)
- EBP accounts for all of the forecasting power of credit spreads for macro activity since the mid-1980s — [NBER Reporter 2018](https://www.nber.org/reporter/2018number4/role-financial-factors-economic-fluctuations)

Breadth
- Zweig Breadth Thrust definition: 10-day EMA of advances/(advances+declines) moving from below 0.40 to above 0.615 within 10 sessions; only 19 signals counted since WWII; it is a bullish (post-crash recovery) signal, not a crash warning; one signal in 2015 led to a maximum gain of only 4.8% (commercial/practitioner sources, small samples) — [LuxAlgo](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/breadth-thrusts/); [Investing.com](https://www.investing.com/analysis/zweig-breadth-thrust-is-it-a-trustworthy-technical-indicator-for-stock-market-200660043); [SentimenTrader](https://sentimentrader.com/blog/a-cluster-of-breadth-thrusts-bodes-well-for-stocks)

Hidden Markov / regime models
- Nystrup et al. (2020a, b) as cited: HMMs are sensitive to mis-estimation and mis-specification (limited samples, unbalanced data, high state persistence, non-normal returns) — [Shu, Yu & Mulvey, arXiv 2402.05272](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.05272v3)
- Shu, Yu & Mulvey (2024): on US, German and Japanese equity indices 1990-2023 with transaction costs and trading delays, statistical jump models beat both buy-and-hold and HMM strategies on US data — [arXiv 2402.05272](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.05272v3)
- Bulla et al. (2011): a Markov-switching strategy dominated static strategies out-of-sample on daily US/Japan/German equity returns (earlier, more favourable evidence) — [MPRA 21154](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/21154/1/MPRA_paper_21154.pdf)

### Inferences
- Robustness ranking for this bot (my judgement from the above): (1) slow trend on SPY and drawdown from peak — robust in direction, modest and cost-sensitive in return; (2) realized volatility level — robust as a description of "risk is high now", contested as a return enhancer; (3) credit spread / EBP from FRED — robust for recession risk, but slow and not shown here to time equities; (4) absorption ratio, turbulence, VIX term structure, breadth, HMM — fragile or contested; suitable only as context lines in the AI explanation.
- Because the exact window barely matters (3-12 months all work gross), the existing crash rule should not be re-tuned to chase a "best" parameter; adding a band or a confirmation count (the bot already uses 10 consecutive closes at the stock level) is the documented way to cut whipsaws.
- With about 90 large US stocks the bot can compute its own breadth (percent above 200-day) and a simple one-eigenvector absorption ratio cheaply; the independent re-check suggests these confirm a crash in progress rather than predict one, so they fit a "severity" display, not a trigger.
- VIX inversion is better read as "panic is already here" (contrarian) than as a reason to sell; that argues against using it to de-risk a long-horizon portfolio.

### Gaps
- Recalled, unverified in this session: Kritzman et al. 2011 use a 500-day window with exponential decay, 51 US industries, standardized shift = (15-day AR minus 1-year AR) / 1-year standard deviation, +/-1 sigma thresholds, and report that all of the worst 1% monthly drawdowns were preceded by a 1-sigma spike. The full text could not be fetched (PDF mirror returned empty).
- Recalled, unverified: Kritzman & Li typically label the top 25% of turbulence readings (75th percentile) as "turbulent". The paper is paywalled; the percentile was not confirmed.
- Recalled, unverified: Moreira & Muir's market-factor alpha of roughly 4.9% a year and Faber's original statistics (1901-2005 S&P 500 timing: similar return, much lower volatility and max drawdown) were not confirmed from primary text.
- No peer-reviewed study was found testing "percent of stocks above the 200-day average" as a crash predictor; breadth evidence found is practitioner-only.
- No study was found that tests credit spreads/EBP or the VIX/VIX3M ratio specifically as an equity market-timing signal with thresholds.
- No source located for a canonical "drawdown from peak" trigger level (e.g. -10% / -20%) with evidence that acting on it helps.

## 2. Does acting on these signals help a long-term investor? Conservative response protocols

### Takeaway
Evidence says timing signals mainly trade a lower drawdown for whipsaw cost and missed rebounds; the benefit is real in long bear markets and absent or negative in fast V-shaped crashes. The best and worst days cluster together, so any exit made during a sell-off has a high chance of missing the rebound; conservative protocols (partial de-risk, bands, rebalance toward target, do nothing) are what large asset owners actually codify.

### Cited Findings
- AQR ("So What If You Miss the Market's N Best Days"): S&P 500 monthly, 1/1970-12/1996, baseline 12.3%; missing best 12 months gives 7.2% (-5.1), missing worst 12 gives 18.1% (+5.8). Out-of-sample 1/1997-4/2024 daily, baseline 9.3%; missing best 30 days gives 2.4% (-6.8), missing worst 30 gives 17.4% (+8.1). The effect is roughly symmetric and largely reproduced with simulated normal returns, so the "miss the best days" chart is not by itself an argument against timing; AQR's conclusion is that timing should be done only in proportion to a real net-of-cost edge and never all-or-nothing — [AQR](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Perspectives/So-What-If-You-Miss-the-Markets-N-Best-Days)
- AQR strategy test in the same piece: an 80/20 mix returned 11.9% (1970-1996); terrible timing 10.3%, great timing 13.5% (about +/-1.6%); out-of-sample 1997-4/2024: 8.7% / 6.9% / 10.6% (about +/-1.8%) — [AQR](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Perspectives/So-What-If-You-Miss-the-Markets-N-Best-Days)
- Clustering: Morningstar found, for the S&P 500 since 1990, the median distance between a worst day and a best day was seven days, and the gain from avoiding worst days roughly offset the loss from missing best days — [Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/funds/you-can-beat-stock-market-by-avoiding-its-worst-days-you-wont)
- Clustering: since 1988 a best or worst day occurred within 21 trading days of another best or worst day more than 70% of the time — [Kiplinger](https://www.kiplinger.com/investing/is-buy-and-hold-really-the-best-investing-strategy)
- Clustering: widely cited J.P. Morgan figure, roughly seven of the 10 best days over about 20 years fell within two weeks of the 10 worst days (six of 10 in an earlier edition) (press report of JPM research) — [CNBC via NBC Los Angeles](https://www.nbclosangeles.com/news/business/money-report/selling-out-during-the-markets-worst-days-can-hurt-you-research-shows-heres-how-much-you-could-lose/3672609/)
- Speed mismatch: in the March 2020 crash trend-following was generally flat while option hedges gained; in the slower 2022 drawdown trend-following held up better — trend protection is slow but persistent (AQR's own perspective, proxy indices) — [AQR Alternative Thinking 2022](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Alternative-Thinking/Tail-Hedging-Strategies); AQR prefers trend to puts as a tail hedge on cost, reliability and practicality — [AQR, Tail Risk Hedging: Contrasting Put and Trend Strategies](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/White-Papers/Tail-Risk-Hedging-Contrasting-Put-and-Trend-Strategies)
- AQR "Working Your Tail Off": direct hedging only pays if one can time short-term crashes AND unwind very shortly afterwards; the authors doubt investors can do either — [AQR](https://www.aqr.com/insights/research/journal-article/working-your-tail-off-active-strategies-vs-direct-hedging)
- Stop-loss theory/evidence: Kaminski & Lo (J. Financial Markets 2014): under a random walk a stop-loss lowers expected return; with momentum it can add value; US equities monthly 1950-2004 with long bonds as the stop-out asset, some stop-loss rules added 50-100 bp per month during stop-out periods (index-level, not single-stock) — [MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/114876)
- Momentum crashes are concentrated in rebounds: they occur in "panic" states after market declines with high volatility and are contemporaneous with market rebounds — [Daniel & Moskowitz, NBER w20439](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20439)
- Asset-owner protocol, Norway GPFG: rule-based rebalancing back to the strategic equity weight when the equity share of the actual benchmark deviates by more than four percentage points, checked on the last trading day of the month (2012-2015 documents; strategic weight then 60%) — [Regjeringen white paper Meld. St. 21 (2014-2015)](https://www.regjeringen.no/en/documents/meld.-st.-21-20142015/id2405360/?ch=10); [NBIM 2012 submission](https://www.nbim.no/en/news-and-insights/submissions-to-ministry/2012/government-pension-fund-global--rules-for-rebalancing-the-benchmark-index/). In 2018 Norges Bank recommended a narrower band of +/-2 percentage points — [NBIM 2018 submission](https://nbim.no/en/publications/submissions-to-ministry/2018/the-rule-for-rebalancing-the-equity-share-in-the-government-pension-fund-global). The equity share is now 70%; the band currently in force was not confirmed.
- Vanguard: for investors not tax-loss harvesting or tracking tightly, annual rebalancing is optimal (2022 "Rational rebalancing") — [Vanguard](https://corporate.vanguard.com/content/dam/corp/research/pdf/rational_rebalancing_analytical_approach_to_multiasset_portfolio_rebalancing.pdf); for its target-date funds a 200 bp trigger / 175 bp destination policy is estimated to add 5-21 bp a year over calendar rebalancing for a hypothetical 60/40 over 10 years (Dec 2024) — [Vanguard, The Rebalancing Edge](https://corporate.vanguard.com/content/dam/corp/research/pdf/the_rebalancing_edge_optimizing_target_date_fund_rebalancing_through_threshold_based_strategies.pdf); a 2015 Vanguard paper found little difference in risk-adjusted return across methods and suggested annual/semi-annual checks with 5% thresholds — [Vanguard 2015 (mirror)](https://journal.tinkoff.ru/media/best-practices-for-portfolio-rebalancing-tlrv.pdf)

### Inferences
- The asset-owner pattern is the opposite of a crash exit: Norway's rule BUYS equities after a large fall (rebalance back up once the band is breached). A defensive switch therefore needs a strong justification; the documented conservative ladder is: do nothing -> pause discretionary new buys / widen review -> partial de-risk -> full defensive only on a slow, confirmed trend break.
- Since the best days sit within about a week of the worst, any crash response that fires on a single large down day is structurally exposed to missing the rebound. Signals should require persistence (weeks, or month-end checks as Faber/NBIM do), and re-entry rules matter as much as exit rules.
- The symmetric AQR result means the bot's crash rule is neither condemned nor justified by the "best days" argument; its value depends on real edge, which the trend literature says is mostly drawdown reduction. Partial sizing (e.g. halving rather than exiting) matches AQR's "in proportion to edge" advice.
- Fast crashes (2020-style) will not be caught by the slow rule; the honest design answer is to accept that and use fast signals only to explain and to widen review, not to trade.

### Gaps
- No quantified whipsaw cost (annual return drag in bull markets) for a 200-day/10-month rule from AQR, Newfound or Alpha Architect was retrieved; only the StockCharts trade count.
- CalPERS drawdown-response or rebalancing policy was not retrieved.
- The current GPFG rebalancing band (4 pp vs 2 pp) could not be confirmed; an undated mandate translation says 2 pp.
- No direct evidence found on "pause new buying" as a protocol; it is an inference from the ladder above.

## 3. Single-stock large-move detection: thresholds and avoiding alert floods

### Takeaway
Academic event studies define a "large move" either by a fixed cut (daily return of at least 10% in absolute value) or by abnormal (market-adjusted) return and volume relative to the stock's own recent history; no vendor or regulator document with standard z-score thresholds was found. For alert hygiene the literature's own practice — measuring abnormal rather than raw return — is the natural way to prevent floods on market-wide days.

### Cited Findings
- Most of the large-price-change literature uses daily declines of at least 10% as the event definition; Bremer & Sweeney (Journal of Finance 1991) study Fortune 500 stocks 1962-1986 and find a significant abnormal rebound over the following days after such one-day drops — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v46y1991i2p747-54.html); [Choi & Jayaraman paper summarising the literature](https://www.scheller.gatech.edu/directory/research/finance/jayaraman/pdf/jfm_overreaction_feb_27_2008.pdf)
- Volume: Gervais, Kaniel & Mingelgrin (Journal of Finance 56(3), 2001) measure a stock's daily/weekly trading volume relative to its own values over the previous several weeks (not raw volume); stocks with unusually high volume subsequently earn higher returns ("high-volume return premium"), linked to visibility (Merton's investor-recognition hypothesis); Kaniel, Ozoguz & Starks (2012) find it generally present in developed markets — [Duke page](https://sites.duke.edu/sgervais/?p=64); [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:56:y:2001:i:3:p:877-919)
- Information classification used in event studies: Chan (JFE 2003) splits stocks by whether a headline appeared in the event month; Savor (JFE 2012) splits large price moves by whether an analyst report was issued at the time — both find the split, not the size of the move, determines what happens next (see section 5) — [Chan, EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:70:y:2003:i:2:p:223-260); [Savor, EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:106:y:2012:i:3:p:635-659)
- Stop-level evidence at stock level inside a momentum portfolio: Han, Zhou & Zhu, "Taming Momentum Crashes" — selling any stock that falls 10% below its start-of-month price, Jan 1926 - Dec 2013, cuts the worst monthly loss of equal-weighted momentum from -49.79% to -11.36% and value-weighted from -64.97% to -23.28% — [Seeking Alpha summary](https://seekingalpha.com/article/3257295-momentum-and-stop-losses). An Alpha Architect replication found the 10% stop's average return about equal to plain momentum (1.27% vs 1.24% monthly) with lower drawdowns and higher Sortino — benefit is in risk, not return — [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/2016/08/taming-the-momentum-roller-coaster-fact-or-fiction/)

### Inferences
- Proposed detection design (my synthesis; thresholds are design choices, not sourced standards):
  - Residual return: r_resid = r_stock - beta x r_SPY (beta from about 1 year of daily data; a sector ETF or the equal-weighted mean of the 90-stock universe can be added as a second factor). Alert on the residual, not the raw return, so that a -4% market day does not produce 90 alerts.
  - Volatility scaling: z = r_resid / sigma_resid, with sigma from a trailing 60-250 day window that EXCLUDES the event day (and ideally uses a robust estimator such as median absolute deviation, so a previous shock does not mute the next alert). Run the same test on 5- and 20-day cumulative residual returns (divide by sigma x sqrt(n)) to catch slow slides.
  - Fat tails: daily stock returns are far from normal, so |z| >= 3 will fire many times a year per stock. Calibrate thresholds empirically on the bot's own 90-stock history to a target alert budget (for example a few alerts per stock per year for "review" tier), instead of trusting normal-distribution probabilities.
  - A fixed-cut backstop consistent with the literature: any raw one-day move of 10% or more is flagged regardless of z.
  - Abnormal volume as a confirmer: volume relative to the stock's own trailing median (Gervais-style), e.g. a multiple of the 20-60 day median; use it to grade the alert (news-like vs noise-like), not as a standalone trigger.
  - Gap: open vs previous close in sigma units identifies overnight news (earnings, guidance); gaps are the events most likely to be "news" moves that drift (section 5).
- Flood control: (a) on days when SPY itself moves beyond its own threshold, emit ONE market-level event and suppress or downgrade per-stock alerts unless the residual z also breaches; (b) cap per-day alerts and rank by |z|; (c) cluster by sector and by day so several semis falling together becomes one "sector" item; (d) cool-down per stock (no re-alert for N days unless the move extends by another threshold step); (e) earnings-day flag so expected-event moves use a wider threshold or a separate channel.
- The existing slow rule (halve after -20% from 52-week high) is a raw-price rule; on market-wide falls it will trigger for many stocks at once. Deciding whether that rule should use market-relative drawdown during a declared market crash state is a design question this evidence raises but does not settle.

### Gaps
- No MSCI/Barra, Bloomberg or other risk-vendor methodology document with specific single-stock alert thresholds (z-score cut-offs, volume multiples) was found; I found no reliable public source stating what thresholds institutional risk systems use.
- No source retrieved for standard event-study abnormal-volume thresholds or for gap-move definitions with evidence.
- Recalled, unverified: exchange single-stock circuit breakers (US Limit Up-Limit Down bands of 5%/10% for large caps over 5 minutes) and market-wide breakers at -7% / -13% / -20% on the S&P 500 are regulatory definitions of "extraordinary" moves; not checked in this session.
- Empirical alert frequency at any z threshold for the bot's universe must be measured on the bot's own data; no published base rate found.

## 4. The upside: "rose too much" signals, bubbles, trimming and concentration

### Takeaway
A very sharp run-up raises the probability of a crash but does not reliably lower expected return, so the evidence supports heightened scrutiny and position-size limits rather than automatic selling. Because a few extreme winners generate most long-run wealth, systematic trimming of winners carries a large hidden cost; the defensible reason to trim is concentration risk, not "it went up too much".

### Cited Findings
- Greenwood, Shleifer & You, "Bubbles for Fama" (NBER w23191, 2017; JFE 131(1), 2019): US industries with a sharp price run-up; a "crash" is a 40% drawdown within two years. Going from a 50% to a 100% industry return net of market over the previous two years raises crash probability from 20% to 53% (19% to 54% in the 2016 draft); at a 150% net-of-market run-up, crash probability is about 80%. But sharp run-ups do NOT predict unusually low average returns going forward — the timing is the problem, since prices often keep rising before the crash — [NBER w23191](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w23191/w23191.pdf); [Shleifer publication page](https://shleifer.scholars.harvard.edu/publications/bubbles-fama)
- LPPL/LPPLS (Sornette): Bree & Joseph (2013) tested Hang Seng crashes 1970-2008; fitted parameters fell inside the ranges proposed by Johansen & Sornette for only 7 of 11 crashes; they argue the posited mechanism does not hold and data support the model only partially — [arXiv 1002.1010](https://arxiv.org/abs/1002.1010v2). The failed 2002 Sornette-Zhou forecast for the US market drew strong objections (Lux 2009; Rosser 2008); the model's ability to estimate the collapse time "is doubtful" per critics — [arXiv 0909.1007 (discussion)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0909.1007). A claimed 0.2% false-positive rate (Lin et al. 2014, against GARCH synthetic data) is disputed as dependent on the null model — [Univ. of Vaasa paper](https://osuva.uwasa.fi/server/api/core/bitstreams/68c09f10-b8b8-406d-8ded-377f5179a0c6/content)
- Skewness: Bessembinder (JFE 129(3), 2018), CRSP 1926-2016: the best-performing 4% of listed companies explain the entire net gain of the US stock market over Treasury bills; most common stocks have lifetime buy-and-hold returns below one-month T-bills — [EconPapers / JFE abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:129:y:2018:i:3:p:440-457). ASU summary: about $35 trillion of net wealth creation came from 1,092 companies (4.3% of nearly 26,000 stocks); 58% of stocks failed to beat T-bills over their lives — [ASU W. P. Carey](https://news.wpcarey.asu.edu/20180917-do-stocks-outperform-treasury-bills)
- Disposition effect: Frazzini (Journal of Finance 2006): investors' tendency to sell winners early causes underreaction to news; stocks with large unrealized capital gains show the strongest post-announcement drift when news and gains have the same sign; an event strategy on this earned monthly alphas above 200 bp — i.e. selling a winner on good news has historically been selling too early — [AQR page for the paper](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/The-Disposition-Effect-and-the-UnderReaction-to-News)
- Rebalancing bands as the institutional answer to drift: see Vanguard and NBIM thresholds in section 2 — [Vanguard](https://corporate.vanguard.com/content/dam/corp/research/pdf/the_rebalancing_edge_optimizing_target_date_fund_rebalancing_through_threshold_based_strategies.pdf); [NBIM](https://www.nbim.no/en/news-and-insights/submissions-to-ministry/2012/government-pension-fund-global--rules-for-rebalancing-the-benchmark-index/)

### Inferences
- A workable "rose too much" detector consistent with Greenwood et al.: trailing 2-year return net of SPY of +100% or more = elevated crash risk (about a coin flip of a 40% drawdown within 2 years at industry level; single stocks are noisier, so treat as indicative). Use it to trigger an AI review and to block ADDING to the position, not to sell.
- The same paper lists features that distinguish run-ups that crashed (recalled: high volatility, new issuance, acceleration, younger firms, turnover); the bot can approximate acceleration and volatility from prices and issuance from SEC filings, which makes this a natural AI-review checklist. (Feature list is recalled, not verified this session.)
- Trimming rule that respects Bessembinder/Frazzini: trim only on position-size breach (weight cap or band around target), and trim back to the cap rather than to the original weight — this limits single-name ruin risk while leaving most of the winner in place. Momentum-based selection with monthly rebalance already drops stocks when their momentum fades, which is a slower, evidence-aligned exit than a "too far, too fast" sale.
- LPPL is not worth implementing: contested out-of-sample, heavy fitting, and unreliable on timing. A simple price-to-trend extreme (e.g. percent above 200-day average, in the stock's own historical percentiles) plus the 2-year net run-up covers the same ground transparently.
- Short-horizon upside spikes: the reversal literature (section 5) finds reversals after large one-day INCREASES weaker or absent, so a one-day jump is not by itself a reason to trim.

### Gaps
- No study retrieved that directly measures the long-run return cost/benefit of a systematic "trim winners" rule in a concentrated long-only portfolio; Akepanidtaworn et al., "Selling Fast and Buying Slow" (recalled: institutional managers' selling decisions underperform random selling, especially sales of extreme winners/losers) was searched for but not retrieved — unverified.
- No sourced numeric convention for single-position caps (e.g. 5%/10% limits) from asset-owner policy documents was retrieved.
- Final published JFE figures for Greenwood et al. may differ slightly from the working-paper numbers quoted.
- No evidence found on "parabolic advance" definitions beyond the run-up thresholds above; chart-pattern definitions are practitioner opinion.

## 5. Post-crash behaviour: reversal vs drift; market-wide vs idiosyncratic; news vs no-news

### Takeaway
The best-documented rule is: large moves accompanied by fundamental news tend to continue (drift), large moves without news tend to reverse; and after market-wide panics the most beaten-down stocks rebound hardest. This argues for classifying a drop (news / no-news, market-wide / idiosyncratic) before responding, and against selling losers mechanically into a market-wide panic.

### Cited Findings
- Chan (JFE 70(2), 2003), headline database: strong drift after bad news; stocks with large moves and no news in the event month tend to reverse the following month; patterns are mainly in smaller, more illiquid stocks — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:70:y:2003:i:2:p:223-260)
- Savor (JFE 106(3), 2012): large price moves accompanied by analyst-report information are followed by drift; no-information moves reverse; drift exists only when the price move and the analyst recommendation change have the same sign; interpretation: investors underreact to fundamental news and overreact to other shocks — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:106:y:2012:i:3:p:635-659); [Penn repository version](https://repository.upenn.edu/handle/20.500.14332/34495)
- Short-term reversal after large one-day drops: Bremer & Sweeney (1991), Fortune 500, 1962-1986, significant abnormal rebound over about three days — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v46y1991i2p747-54.html). Atkins & Dyl (JFQA 1990): reversals not significant after bid-ask bounce and transaction costs. Cox & Peterson (Journal of Finance 1994), 1963-June 1991: three-day reversal, no support for the De Bondt-Thaler overreaction hypothesis, and the reversal faded over time. Reversals after large INCREASES are weaker or absent (Atkins & Dyl 1990; Bremer, Hiraki & Sweeney 1997) (all via secondary summary) — [Choi & Jayaraman literature summary](https://www.scheller.gatech.edu/directory/research/finance/jayaraman/pdf/jfm_overreaction_feb_27_2008.pdf)
- Post-earnings-announcement drift today: Martineau (Critical Finance Review 2022, "Rest in Peace PEAD"): for large stocks PEAD has been non-existent since 2006; prices fully reflect earnings surprises on the announcement date — [Critical Finance Review](https://nowpublishers.com/article/Details/CFR-0122). Contested: 2025 work (Dickerson, Julliard & Mueller; a Subrahmanyam working paper) argues drift remains important and that disagreement reflects research-design choices — [UCLA Anderson Review](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/is-post-earnings-announcement-drift-a-thing-again)
- Market-wide panics: Daniel & Moskowitz (JFE 122(2), 2016): momentum crashes occur in "panic" states (after market declines, with high volatility) and coincide with market rebounds; past losers have low down-market betas but very large up-market betas in these states, so they "perform magnificently" in the rebound while winners lag; robust across eight markets/asset classes; scaling momentum exposure down when volatility is high mitigates it — [NBER w20439](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20439); [Chicago Booth Review](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/understanding-momentum-crashes) (worst months for the strategy: July and August 1932)
- Disposition/underreaction link: drift after news is strongest where holders sit on gains (good news) or losses (bad news) of the same sign — [Frazzini 2006 via AQR](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/The-Disposition-Effect-and-the-UnderReaction-to-News)

### Inferences
- Decision table for the bot (synthesis):
  - Idiosyncratic drop + identifiable fundamental news (earnings miss, guidance cut, 8-K, fraud, regulatory action): expect continuation rather than bounce -> escalate to heavy AI thesis review promptly; do not "buy the dip"; the existing slow halve rule is directionally consistent with drift.
  - Idiosyncratic drop + no news found: reversal is more likely; reversal evidence is short-horizon (days to a month), small after costs, and weaker in large caps -> informational alert only, no action; re-check if it extends over 5-20 days.
  - Market-wide drop (residual small): stock-level information content is low; hardest-hit names rebound most in the recovery -> suppress per-stock action, let the market-level rule govern, and specifically avoid stock-level halving cascades at the panic low.
  - Large rise + news: drift likely continues (Savor, Frazzini) -> no trim; large rise + no news: weak evidence of reversal -> informational.
- For the bot's ~90 large caps, both reversal (illiquid-stock effect) and PEAD (gone for large caps since 2006 per Martineau, contested) are weak; the practical value of news classification is in routing to AI review, not in expected-return prediction.
- The bot's own momentum selection is exposed to Daniel-Moskowitz crash risk precisely after its market crash rule would have fired: at re-entry after a panic, the momentum list is loaded with low-beta "winners" that lag the rebound. Worth flagging to the designer as a known interaction (possible mitigation: scale or delay momentum re-ranking when trailing market return is negative and volatility is high).
- The bot has news headlines and SEC filings, so a cheap news/no-news flag (any headline or 8-K within the event window) is implementable and is exactly the Chan/Savor conditioning variable.

### Gaps
- De Bondt & Thaler (1985) long-horizon (3-5 year) loser reversal: primary source not retrieved; only mentioned via Cox & Peterson's rejection at short horizons. Recalled, unverified: 3-year loser portfolios outperformed winners by about 25% over the following 3 years (1926-1982), concentrated in January and small stocks.
- Daniel & Moskowitz numbers could not be extracted (PDF binary). Recalled, unverified: bear-state defined as negative trailing 24-month market return; in March-May 2009 the loser decile rose about 163% vs about 8% for winners; dynamic volatility-scaled momentum roughly doubles the Sharpe ratio.
- Tetlock ("Does Public Financial News Resolve Asymmetric Information?", RFS 2010) on reversal being smaller on news days was searched but not retrieved.
- No study retrieved quantifying recovery time of individual large caps after -20%/-30% idiosyncratic drops (base rates for "does it come back").

## 6. Institutional escalation: alert tiers, responders, deadlines, documentation

### Takeaway
Public sources describe a common skeleton — a monitored utilisation level, a soft (amber) threshold that requires review and a hard (red) limit that requires action, with documented escalation paths and named owners — but specific deadlines are firm-specific and I found no authoritative asset-manager standard with numbers.

### Cited Findings
- Boston Fed working paper (2024, bank data): firms typically target risk-limit utilisation of 70-80% and use two thresholds, soft (amber) and hard (red), to manage mitigating actions; red breaches are rare, and thresholds are sometimes adjusted in anticipation of a breach — [Boston Fed SRA 24-01](https://www.bostonfed.org/-/media/Documents/Workingpapers/PDF/2024/sra2401.pdf)
- A limit framework typically specifies the frequency of review of limit utilisation and the escalation procedure in case of a breach — [Open Risk Manual](https://www.openriskmanual.org/wiki/Limit_framework)
- Supervisory expectation: the risk governance framework should set out escalation and notification procedures with clearly defined roles of risk owners — [HKMA Supervisory Policy Manual IC-1](https://brdr.hkma.gov.hk/chi/doc-ldg/docId/getPdf/20171006-3-EN/IC-1.pdf)
- Buy-side principles exist (GARP Buy Side Risk Managers Forum, "Risk Principles for Asset Managers"); the IACPM 2024 white paper notes most firms treat concentration limits as hard limits tied to formal policy (details not read in full) — [GARP](https://www.garp.org/hubfs/Website/BSRMF/PDF/risk-principles-asset-managers.pdf); [IACPM](https://iacpm.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/IACPM-Research-Concentration-Limit-Frameworks-White-Paper.pdf)
- Illustrative (NOT authoritative) template: warning at about 75% utilisation; soft breach -> notify CRO/desk head within one hour, remediation plan the same business day; hard breach -> immediate notification and a defined timeframe to reduce the position; timeframes in hours and roles named explicitly — [skills.lc template](https://www.skills.lc/CaseMark/skills/casemark-skills-skills-finance-managing-market-risk-limits-skill-md)
- Asset-owner practice is rule-first: Norway's rebalancing is triggered mechanically at a month-end check against a published band, removing discretion at the moment of stress — [Regjeringen Meld. St. 21 (2014-2015)](https://www.regjeringen.no/en/documents/meld.-st.-21-20142015/id2405360/?ch=10)

### Inferences
- Mapping to a one-person bot (synthesis; deadlines are design choices):
  - Tier 0, log only: moves inside normal range, or stock moves explained by the market on a market-wide day. No notification; visible on the site.
  - Tier 1, informational: residual move beyond the lower threshold, no news, or price-to-trend extreme. One line in the daily digest; no response required.
  - Tier 2, requires review: residual move beyond the upper threshold, or any large move WITH news, or 2-year net run-up of +100%, or position-weight band breached. Triggers the per-stock AI review; user acknowledges within a stated window (days, not hours — long-horizon).
  - Tier 3, requires action: pre-committed rules only (market crash rule fires; per-stock -20% slow rule; hard position cap). The action is mechanical and already decided; the alert states the share list and the reason.
- Borrow three institutional habits: (1) thresholds and responses are written down BEFORE the event (pre-commitment, as in the GPFG mandate); (2) every Tier 2/3 event records what fired, the data at the time, the decision taken and the reason for any deviation from the rule — this matches the bot's existing deviation rules and snapshot ledger; (3) amber/red separation, so that most alerts are review-only and action-level events stay rare (the Boston Fed finding that red breaches are rare is a sanity check on calibration: if Tier 3 fires often, thresholds are wrong).
- A periodic review of alert counts per tier (alert budget) is the practical equivalent of "frequency of review of limit utilisation".

### Gaps
- No authoritative buy-side source with concrete escalation deadlines or documentation templates was retrieved; GARP and IACPM documents were located but not read in full.
- NBIM and CalPERS internal risk-escalation procedures (as opposed to rebalancing rules) were not found.
- The one source with explicit hour-level deadlines is a non-authoritative template and is bank/trading-desk oriented; it should not be cited as industry standard.

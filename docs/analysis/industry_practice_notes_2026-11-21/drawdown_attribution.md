## 1. How do factor risk models decompose a single stock's return, and what is the minimal version?

### Takeaway
Every commercial fundamental model uses the same shape: stock return = market (country) factor + industry factor(s) + style factors + a "specific" residual that is whatever the cross-sectional regression does not explain. The minimal individual-investor version is a time-series analogue: beta x market return, then peer-group return net of market, then the residual. It needs only daily prices, but it cannot separate style effects (momentum, growth, volatility) from the residual.

### Cited Findings
- Barra-style models split a stock's return into a country factor, industry factors and style factors, with specific return defined as the part the factors do not explain; the China model CNE5 has 1 country factor, 10 style factors and 32 industry factors. [snippet] — [arXiv 2411.04409 describing CNE5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.04409)
- Barra USE4 (US model) has 60 industry factors based on GICS and 12 style factors "to model risk sources not captured by the market or industry factors". [primary] — [MSCI, Barra US Equity Model USE4 factsheet](https://www.msci.com/documents/10199/242721/Barra_US_Equity_Model_USE4.pdf)
- USE4's 12 style factors are: Beta, Non-linear Beta, Liquidity, Residual Volatility, Momentum, Size, Non-linear Size, Book-to-Price, Growth, Leverage, Dividend Yield, Earnings Yield. Residual Volatility "explains returns associated with high-volatility stocks that are not captured by the Beta factor". [primary] — [MSCI USE4 factsheet](https://www.msci.com/documents/10199/242721/Barra_US_Equity_Model_USE4.pdf)
- USE4 explicitly lists "Separation of market effect from industries" as a key feature, "resulting in increased forecast accuracy and greater explanatory power with regards to the industry factor returns, ex-market". [primary] — [MSCI USE4 factsheet](https://www.msci.com/documents/10199/242721/Barra_US_Equity_Model_USE4.pdf)
- In the previous model (USE3) the cap-weighted market was style-neutral by construction, so the market's return was carried entirely by the industry factors; USE4 added an explicit market (country) factor and a constrained regression for factor returns. [snippet] — [MSCI USE4 document, as summarized in search](https://www.msci.com/documents/10199/242721/Barra_US_Equity_Model_USE4.pdf)
- USE4 exposures (technical exposures, fundamental factors, industry membership) are updated daily, with daily model history back to July 1995, and the model ships in Daily, Short and Long forecast-horizon versions. A "Volatility Regime Adjustment" calibrates factor volatilities to current market levels to reduce under-prediction of risk when entering a high-volatility regime. [primary] — [MSCI USE4 factsheet](https://www.msci.com/documents/10199/242721/Barra_US_Equity_Model_USE4.pdf)
- USE4 allows a firm to be mapped to multiple industries ("multi-industry scheme"), consistent with GICS. [primary] — [MSCI USE4 factsheet](https://www.msci.com/documents/10199/242721/Barra_US_Equity_Model_USE4.pdf)
- Barra's regional model (EUE3) treats industry, country and style factors as offsets to a regional market factor and estimates factor returns by robust cross-sectional regression; the global model (GEM2) has a World factor and imposes no hierarchy between country and industry factors. [snippet] — [MSCI EUE3 document](https://www.msci.com/documents/10199/248121/Barra0Europe0Equity0Model0(EUE3)/549ffb8a-19a5-41e5-b237-64335efbea6a); [GEM2 factsheet](https://app2-nv.msci.com/products/analytics/models/global_equity_model/GEM2_factsheet.pdf)
- Axioma's fundamental model follows the same principle: factor exposures are given, factor returns are solved for with weighted multivariate cross-sectional regression, and specific return is the remainder. Its sector factors are described as returns "in excess of the style and market factors", i.e. sector is measured net of market. [snippet] — [Axioma/SimCorp statistical models paper](https://view.ceros.com/simcorp/axwp-statistical-models-r2-pdf); [FactSet Insight on Axioma's macroeconomic model](https://insight.factset.com/axiomas-macroeconomic-model-insights-into-equity-portfolios-from-a-new-perspective)
- Bloomberg's MAC3 model is positioned as separating idiosyncratic from factor returns, with style and industry factors for equities; Bloomberg says country and industry betas replace dummy (0/1) variables in its factor design. [snippet] — [Bloomberg MAC3 product page](https://www.bloomberg.com/professional/products/risk/mac3)
- MSCI measures how much of the common-factor part of return dispersion comes from each layer: for MSCI Europe IMI, Jan 2002 to Sep 2021, sectors averaged 51%, style factors 34% and countries 15% of the common-factor contribution to cross-sectional volatility; style factors' share has risen and factors "tend to matter more during severe market stress" (2008, 2011, start of COVID-19). The page does not state the stock-specific share. [primary] — [MSCI, "Have Sectors Driven Stock Returns?" (Lodh, Harris, Kassianidis, 19 Jan 2022)](https://www.msci.com/research-and-insights/quick-take/have-sectors-driven-stock-returns)
- Academic time-series version of the three-layer split: Campbell, Lettau, Malkiel and Xu decompose return volatility into market, industry and firm-level components using daily data (July 1962 to Dec 1997); market and industry variance were stable while firm-level variance more than doubled, and the explanatory power of the market model for a typical stock declined. [snippet] — [NBER Digest summary](https://www.nber.org/digest/jun00/individual-stocks-have-become-more-volatile); [NBER WP 7590](https://www.nber.org/papers/w7590)
- A 2022 follow-up review exists ("Idiosyncratic Equity Risk Two Decades Later", NBER WP 29916); not opened. — [NBER WP 29916](https://nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29916/w29916.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Two different constructions carry the same name.** Vendor models are *cross-sectional*: each day they regress all stocks' returns on that day's exposures, so the market/industry/style factor returns are estimated from thousands of stocks. The individual-investor version is *time-series*: regress one stock's history on SPY (and a peer index). The layers mean the same thing but the residuals differ; a time-series residual still contains all style effects.
- **Minimal three-layer recipe that needs only daily closes for ~90 stocks plus SPY:**
  1. Market layer = beta_i x r_SPY, with beta_i from a rolling regression on prior days only.
  2. Peer layer = gamma_i x (peer-group return minus the peers' own beta-implied return), where the peer return is computed leaving stock i out.
  3. Residual = actual return minus (1) minus (2).
  Mirroring USE4's design choice, the peer layer should be measured *net of market* (Axioma and USE4 both define sector net of market), otherwise the market move gets counted twice.
- **What the minimal version cannot do:** separate style effects. On a day when high-momentum or high-volatility names all fall together across sectors, the minimal model shows it as many simultaneous "idiosyncratic" residuals of the same sign. A cheap proxy check is the sign and breadth of residuals across the 90 names: genuinely stock-specific events are, by definition, not correlated across unrelated stocks.
- USE4 includes both Beta and Residual Volatility as style factors on top of a market factor, which is the vendor's acknowledgement that a single linear beta does not fully capture how high-beta, high-volatility stocks behave in sell-offs. A one-beta model will systematically under-explain such stocks' drops.
- For ~90 large US stocks, several are themselves large SPY constituents, so "market return" partly contains the stock. This biases beta toward 1 and shrinks the residual for mega-caps. (My inference; no source checked.)

### Gaps
- The exact USE4 regression specification (regression weights, the constraint tying industry factor returns to the market factor, half-lives) is in the USE4 Methodology Notes, which I could not retrieve; only the factsheet was read. Background knowledge says cap-weighted industry factor returns are constrained to sum to zero and the regression is weighted by square root of market cap, but this was **not verified in this session**.
- No Northfield methodology document was found.
- I found no vendor figure for the typical share of a *single stock's daily* variance explained by market vs. industry vs. style vs. specific. The MSCI figure above is a split of the common-factor part of cross-sectional dispersion in Europe, not a single-stock R-squared.
- Fama-French style time-series factor decomposition (using published daily factor returns from Kenneth French's library as free extra regressors) was not researched; it is a plausible free middle ground between "SPY only" and a commercial model.

## 2. How do multi-manager "pod" hedge funds use idiosyncratic vs. factor P&L attribution?

### Takeaway
The documented core idea is that a pod PM is paid for idiosyncratic (residual) return and is required to hedge or cap market, sector and style exposure, with the factor model run continuously on the book. Almost all specific numbers in circulation (drawdown stops, exposure bands) come from unsourced blogs; I found no primary disclosure from Millennium, Citadel, Point72 or Balyasny, and no documented account of what a PM does differently when a loss is idiosyncratic vs. factor-driven.

### Cited Findings
- The most citable documented source is Giuseppe Paleologo's *Advanced Portfolio Management: A Quant's Guide for Fundamental Investors* (Wiley, 2021), written for fundamental PMs. The publisher describes it as covering how to separate stock-specific return drivers from the investment environment's return drivers, position sizing, and understanding performance. — [Wiley](https://www.wiley.com/en-ca/shop/general-finance-investments/advanced-portfolio-management-a-quant's-guide-for-fundamental-investors-p-9781119789802)
- The book has a chapter "Manage Factor Risk" (incl. "Tactical Factor Risk Management") and a section on "Percentage Idio Variance"; an excerpt recommends setting risk limits on %idio variance, style and factor variance "compatible with your investment style". It also notes factor-mimicking (hedge) portfolios themselves carry idiosyncratic risk. [snippet] — [book excerpt listing](https://dokumen.pub/advanced-portfolio-management-a-quants-guide-for-fundamental-investors-1nbsped-1119789796-9781119789796.html); [Google Books](https://books.google.com/books/about/Advanced_Portfolio_Management.html?id=-yk1EAAAQBAJ)
- A practitioner summary states "risk models increasingly drive the behavior of fundamental long short equity investors" and that the factor-return time series drives residual returns, mimicking portfolios and idio-variance percentages. [snippet] — [Rich Falk-Wallace on X](https://x.com/richfalkwallace/status/1799414735848300740)
- [lore] A long position is decomposed into market, sector, growth, momentum, size, quality and an idiosyncratic residual; the PM is paid for the residual and other exposures are hedged with futures, ETF shorts, factor baskets or pair trades. Factor models run continuously; PMs see net factor exposures in real time and risk officers see the aggregate across pods. Barra and Axioma are named alongside proprietary systems. The article cites no sources. — [Quant Enthusiasts Substack, 10 May 2026](https://youngandcalculated.substack.com/p/risk-management-inside-a-pod-how)
- [lore] Stacked limits reported by the same article: single name 1-3% of gross book; GICS industry 5-15% of gross; 5-10% of 30-day average daily volume; net exposure roughly -10% to +10%; plus beta-adjusted gross, per-factor exposure limits and VaR/CVaR contribution limits. — [Quant Enthusiasts Substack](https://youngandcalculated.substack.com/p/risk-management-inside-a-pod-how)
- [lore] Drawdown rules differ by source: (a) forced de-risking around 5% and wind-down/termination at 7.5-10% at Millennium, with the first cut applied mechanically by the risk team — [Quant Enthusiasts Substack](https://youngandcalculated.substack.com/p/risk-management-inside-a-pod-how); (b) 5% drawdown halves capital, 7.5% terminates the pod — [Medium article](https://medium.com/@navnoorbawa/the-pod-shop-revolution-how-multi-strategy-funds-generated-13-6-returns-while-markets-stumbled-8e090d97b68b); (c) soft trigger about 5% of allocated capital, hard stop about 8-10%, varying by firm and pod — [Risk Hub](https://riskhub.org/blogs/multi-strategy-hedge-funds). None is a firm disclosure.
- [lore] Firms differ in centralization: Citadel is described as sharing formats, styles, ideas and analysis between pods, Millennium as the opposite and more aggressive about closing pods in drawdown. — [Rupak Ghose, "Citadel is from Mars and Millennium is from Venus"](https://rupakghose.substack.com/p/citadel-is-from-mars-and-millennium)
- [lore] Critical view: in stressed markets several pods can approach stop-loss limits together, and fixed stop-loss rules may not distinguish skill from noise (summary of a working paper attributed to Marcos Mollica). — [CB Partners](https://www.cb-partners.com/projects/pod-platforms)

### Inferences
- The transferable idea is **not** the stop-loss machinery (which exists because pods run leverage and short horizons) but the accounting discipline: every day, every position's P&L is pre-sorted into "explained by exposures everyone shares" and "residual", and human attention is spent only on the residual. A long-only, long-horizon bot can borrow exactly that triage without borrowing the stops.
- In a pod, factor-driven loss is a *risk-management* problem (hedge was wrong or missing) handled at book level, while idiosyncratic loss is a *thesis* problem handled at name level. For a long-only bot with no hedging, the analogous split is: factor/market-driven drop → one shared market-level explanation, no per-stock thesis re-check; idiosyncratic drop → per-stock fundamental re-check. This mapping is my inference; I found no source that documents the decision rule in those words.
- Mechanical drawdown stops are designed for short-horizon, levered books. They are evidence of how seriously pods take residual vs. factor separation, not a template for a long-horizon holder.

### Gaps
- No primary source (investor letter, regulatory filing, on-record interview with a risk head or PM) was found for any firm's limits or daily workflow. An Odd Lots podcast episode on multi-strategy funds appeared in results but was not opened.
- "What does a PM do when the loss is idiosyncratic vs. factor-driven?" is not answered by any source I could read; the one article that might cover it is paywalled past the position-sizing section.
- No documented target for idiosyncratic share of risk (figures such as "70-80%+ idio variance" circulate as lore; I could not source one).
- Paleologo's exact definition and recommended level of %idio variance should be taken from the book itself (around p. 170 per the excerpt listing).

## 3. How do long-only fundamental managers use performance attribution in reviewing losers?

### Takeaway
The standard long-only tool is Brinson attribution (allocation, selection, interaction against a benchmark by sector), increasingly supplemented by factor-based attribution because Brinson's "stock selection" bucket turns out to contain a lot of unintended style exposure. Both are portfolio-level, period-level tools; I found no documented institutional procedure that uses them as a per-stock, per-day trigger for reviewing a loser.

### Cited Findings
- Brinson attribution decomposes value added versus a benchmark into allocation, selection and interaction effects; the CFA/CIPM curriculum covers Brinson-Hood-Beebower and Brinson-Fachler and compares asset-grouping (Brinson) models with factor models, including each one's advantages and disadvantages. — [CFA Institute CIPM reading](https://www.cfainstitute.org/-/media/documents/support/programs/cipm/2019-cipm-l1v1r5.ashx); [CFA Institute Research Foundation literature review on performance attribution](https://www.cfainstitute.org/-/media/documents/book/rf-lit-review/2019/rflr-performance-attribution.pdf)
- Many managers report externally with a two-effect model, folding interaction into selection. — [FactSet Insight, "Equity attribution and the delicate art of interaction"](https://insight.factset.com/equity-attribution-and-the-delicate-art-of-interaction)
- MSCI (Davis and Menchero) built a factor model that reproduces classic Brinson results and found that much of the Brinson "stock selection effect" is actually attributable to a handful of style factors. [snippet] — [MSCI, "Beyond Brinson"](https://www.msci.com/www/research-report/beyond-brinson-establishing-the/015322069)
- FactSet's worked example: of 390 bp of excess return, a multi-factor framework assigns 292 bp to systematic factor exposures and 99 bp to stock-specific decisions. [snippet] — [FactSet Insight, multi-factor attribution](https://insight.factset.com/how-a-multi-factor-attribution-framework-can-provide-a-deeper-insight-into-the-sources-of-relative-performance_)
- MSCI's analysis of active funds in 2022 found style-factor and industry exposures led performance, with stock selection contributing about 25% for top-decile funds. [snippet] — [MSCI, "Style Bets, Not Stock Picks, Drove Returns in 2022"](https://www.msci.com/research-and-insights/blog-post/style-bets-not-stock-picks-drove-returns-in-2022)
- Hentschel (2024) argues the Brinson interaction term plays a role analogous to residuals in a factor model. [snippet] — [Hentschel 2024](https://www.ludgerhentschel.com/PDFs/Hentschel%20'24b.pdf)
- Holdings-based attribution is the fallback when transactions are unavailable. — [Morningstar Equity Performance Attribution Methodology](https://morningstardirect.morningstar.com/clientcomm/Morningstar-Equity-Performance-Attribution-Methodology.pdf)
- An open implementation of both Brinson and regression-based attribution for long-only, single-currency equity portfolios exists (R package `pa`). — [R Journal, "Performance Attribution for Equity Portfolios"](https://journal.r-project.org/archive/2013-2/lu-kane.pdf)
- Retail-level guidance (not institutional): event-driven thesis reviews should fire on defined triggers such as earnings, management change or a valuation threshold, and daily price movement is deliberately not a trigger "because price alone is not evidence". — [Kubera, investment thesis guide](https://www.kubera.com/blog/investment-thesis)

### Inferences
- The consistent finding across MSCI and FactSet is a warning that applies directly to reviewing losers: **what looks like a stock-picking outcome is often a style/industry outcome.** A loser review that starts from the raw price drop will over-attribute to the company.
- Brinson needs benchmark sector weights and portfolio weights; it answers "was my sector mix or my picks responsible this quarter", which is a periodic portfolio review question, not a daily trigger question. For the bot, the Brinson idea transfers as a *periodic* report (e.g. quarterly: how much of each holding's relative return was peer-group vs. within-peer-group), not as the daily gate.
- The per-stock analogue of "selection effect" is simply the stock's return minus its peer group's return over the review period. That number, tracked cumulatively, is what a long-horizon reviewer would look at for a persistent loser.

### Gaps
- No documented long-only firm process was found that states "we re-underwrite a holding when X". Searches returned only retail blog content; treat any specific trigger rule as unsourced.
- No source found on decision-quality/post-mortem practice (process vs. outcome reviews) at long-only managers.
- Stock-level contribution/attribution is covered in attribution textbooks (e.g. a Singer volume appeared in results) but content was not read.

## 4. Event-study methodology: windows, significance thresholds, pitfalls on volatile days and for high-beta stocks

### Takeaway
The standard recipe is: estimate a market model (alpha, beta) on a pre-event window that excludes the event, compute abnormal return = actual minus alpha minus beta x market, and compare it to the residual standard deviation from the estimation window, conventionally at the 5% level. For a single stock on a single day this test is known to be unreliable: residuals are fat-tailed, volatility is not constant, standard errors are understated in turbulent markets (too many false positives), and at the same time the test has low power.

### Cited Findings
- Abnormal return is the actual return over the event window minus the normal (expected) return; two common normal-return models are the constant-mean model and the market model R_it = alpha_i + beta_i R_mt + e_it, the latter being "most commonly used" and assuming a stable linear relation between market and security return. [primary] — [Kellogg PhD lecture notes on event studies (after Campbell-Lo-MacKinlay ch. 4 and MacKinlay 1997)](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/sapienza/ftp/Ph.D.Teaching/ECF/EmpCF_Lecture5.pdf)
- The market model improves on the constant-mean model because removing the market-related part of the return reduces the variance of the abnormal return and so raises the ability to detect event effects; the size of the benefit depends on the model's R-squared. [primary] — [Kellogg lecture notes](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/sapienza/ftp/Ph.D.Teaching/ECF/EmpCF_Lecture5.pdf)
- Popular market proxies are the S&P 500 and CRSP value- or equal-weighted indexes. Procedure: estimate by OLS in the estimation window, then apply the estimated parameters to the event window. The estimation window excludes the event window and the two typically do not overlap. [primary] — [Kellogg lecture notes](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/sapienza/ftp/Ph.D.Teaching/ECF/EmpCF_Lecture5.pdf)
- MacKinlay (1997): the most common choice is to use the period prior to the event for estimation; "in an event study using daily data and the market model, the market model parameters could be estimated over the 120 days prior to the event." The event period is excluded so the event does not contaminate the normal-return model. [snippet] — [MacKinlay 1997, J. Econ. Lit. 35(1):13-39, hosted copy](https://eclass.aueb.gr/modules/document/file.php/ODE289/%CE%91%CE%A1%CE%98%CE%A1%CE%91%20-%20%CE%94%CE%97%CE%9C%CE%9F%CE%A3%CE%99%CE%95%CE%A5%CE%A3%CE%95%CE%99%CE%A3/%CE%94%CE%99%CE%91%CE%9B%CE%95%CE%9E%CE%97%201%20-%202/EFFICIENT%20MARKET%20HYPOTHESIS%20MAC%20KINLAY%201997.pdf) (login-walled when fetched); [Wikipedia, Event study](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Event_study)
- Practice variations: an estimation window ending about a month before the event (e.g. days -120 to -31) to avoid anticipation effects. [snippet] — [Pacicco et al.](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/340256/files/Pacicco.pdf)
- Event windows are short: the announcement day, plus the next day when news arrives after the close; typical examples are [-1,0] and a 21-day (-10,+10) window. Multi-day moves are summed into a cumulative abnormal return (CAR) whose variance scales with the number of days. [primary] — [Kellogg lecture notes](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/sapienza/ftp/Ph.D.Teaching/ECF/EmpCF_Lecture5.pdf)
- Daily-data beta estimates can be biased by non-synchronous trading; classic studies used the Scholes-Williams (1977) correction. [primary] — [Kellogg lecture notes](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/sapienza/ftp/Ph.D.Teaching/ECF/EmpCF_Lecture5.pdf)
- Listed criticisms: confounding events (the longer the window, the harder to rule them out), anticipation/leakage, and test statistics that rest on normality (non-parametric alternatives: sign/binomial test, Wilcoxon signed-rank). [primary] — [Kellogg lecture notes](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/sapienza/ftp/Ph.D.Teaching/ECF/EmpCF_Lecture5.pdf)
- When event windows overlap in calendar time, abnormal returns are cross-correlated and the usual variance is wrong. [primary] — [Kellogg lecture notes](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/sapienza/ftp/Ph.D.Teaching/ECF/EmpCF_Lecture5.pdf). Kolari and Pynnonen (RFS 2010) show even low cross-correlation causes serious over-rejection when event dates cluster (a correlation as small as 0.05 is reported to bias tests). [snippet] — [SSRN abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=1830364)
- Event-induced variance (volatility itself jumps on event days) is the subject of Boehmer, Musumeci and Poulsen (1991), and the problem intensifies when combined with cross-sectional correlation. [snippet] — [RePEc entry for Kolari-Pynnonen 2010](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v23y2010i11p3996-4025.html)
- Significance convention: academic event studies use a 95% standard (false-positive rate under 5%). [primary] — [Fisch, Gelbach and Klick, CLS Blue Sky Blog, 12 Sep 2016](https://clsbluesky.law.columbia.edu/2016/09/12/toward-a-better-understanding-of-event-studies-in-securities-litigation/)
- Single-firm, single-day pitfalls identified by the same authors: (a) the standard method assumes normally distributed excess returns "even though it is well documented that this is often not the case"; (b) it assumes the same standard deviation on all dates though volatility varies; (c) it ignores multiple testing across dates; (d) direction matters, so one-sided tests are appropriate; (e) applying the 95% standard to one firm yields "a very high frequency of false negatives" (low power). [primary] — [CLS Blue Sky Blog](https://clsbluesky.law.columbia.edu/2016/09/12/toward-a-better-understanding-of-event-studies-in-securities-litigation/)
- Proposed fix for non-normality: the SQ test, which uses sample quantiles of the empirical distribution of pre-event fitted excess returns instead of a normal critical value. [snippet] — [Gelbach, Helland and Klick 2013, "Valid Inference in Single-Firm, Single-Event Studies"](https://www.rand.org/pubs/external_publications/EP68377.html)
- High-volatility markets: Baker (2016) finds that in the presence of broad market volatility a standard event study flags too many returns as significant because standard errors are underestimated; he proposes a VIX-adjusted OLS model and FGLS. [snippet] — [Stanford Law Review, vol. 68 issue 5](https://www.stanfordlawreview.org/print/article/single-firm-event-studies-securities-fraud-and-financial-crisis-problems-inference/)
- Baker's conference slides report Dow-30 rejection frequencies at a nominal 5% level of roughly 5.4-7.0% for standard OLS, 5.1-9.1% for FGLS in the crisis period, and 4.4-5.1% for VIX-adjusted OLS; the row labels were ambiguous in the extracted text, so verify before quoting. [snippet] — [Baker, ALEA 2016 slides](https://andrewcbaker.netlify.com/talk/alea_2016/ALEA_2016.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Mapping to the bot:** every daily "is this drop stock-specific?" check is a single-firm, single-day event study, i.e. the weakest setting in the literature. The three corrections with the best support and lowest cost are: (1) use the stock's own empirical residual quantiles rather than a "2 sigma" normal rule (SQ-test idea); (2) scale the residual volatility to the current regime rather than using a flat 120-day sigma (Baker's VIX-adjustment idea; Barra's Volatility Regime Adjustment is the same idea at vendor scale); (3) test one-sided (only drops matter).
- **Multiple-testing arithmetic (mine):** with 90 stocks tested daily at a nominal 5% two-sided normal threshold, about 4.5 flags per day are expected with no news at all, more given fat tails; at a 1% one-sided threshold about 0.9 per day. A threshold has to be chosen against a budget of AI reviews per day, not against textbook significance.
- **High-beta stocks:** beta-times-market is a linear, constant-beta approximation. On large down days betas tend to be unstable and estimation error in beta is multiplied by a large market move, so the residual of a high-beta stock is noisiest exactly when it matters. (General reasoning; the supporting evidence here is indirect: USE4 adds Non-linear Beta and Residual Volatility factors, and Baker shows over-rejection in volatile markets.) Practical consequence: on days when |market move| is large, require a larger residual before calling a move idiosyncratic, or express the residual in units of regime-scaled sigma.
- **Clustering:** on a broad sell-off day all 90 "events" share one calendar date, so the residuals are cross-correlated (Kolari-Pynnonen). Many simultaneous same-sign residuals should be read as evidence of a missing common factor, not as many independent company events.
- **Multi-day drawdowns:** a slow slide never trips a daily threshold. CAR over 5/20/60 days, compared with sigma x sqrt(days), is the standard way to catch it; the confounding-event problem grows with window length.
- Estimation choices that fit the literature and daily data: roughly 120-250 trading days ending some days before the test date, the test day excluded, and known event days (earnings) either excluded from estimation or accepted as part of the stock's normal fat tails.

### Gaps
- I could not open MacKinlay (1997) or Campbell-Lo-MacKinlay directly, so the paper's own worked example (event/estimation window lengths) and power tables are not quoted. ## 5. How should peer groups be defined without a sector data vendor, and what are the pitfalls?

### Takeaway
For large-cap US stocks the published evidence favours standard industry classifications (GICS, Fama-French) over purely statistical return clustering, with useful resolution down to about the 6-digit GICS (industry) level; text-based peers (Hoberg-Phillips) capture comovement that fixed classifications miss. The main pitfall, which no classification solves, is that a "peer group" move can itself be the story.

### Cited Findings
- Chan, Lakonishok and Swaminathan (FAJ 2007): both GICS and Fama-French classifications produce groups with larger within-group return correlations and sharper discrimination against outside-group correlations than groups formed by statistical cluster analysis. [snippet] — [CFA Institute / Financial Analysts Journal page](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/2007/industry-classifications-and-return-comovement); [LSV-hosted paper](https://www.lsvasset.com/pdf/research-papers/industry_FAJ.pdf)
- Same paper: industry comovement is stronger for large companies than small ones. Large-cap stocks in the same 2-digit GICS sector have a mean return correlation of 0.38 versus 0.26 for stocks in different sectors; after removing an equal-weighted market index, same-industry pairs still correlate about 0.17. [snippet; verify numbers against the paper] — [FAJ 2007](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/2007/industry-classifications-and-return-comovement)
- Same paper: finer disaggregation improves discrimination up to 6-digit GICS codes, after which benefits tail off. Operating performance also clusters (large companies in the same 4-digit GICS group: sales-growth correlation 0.22 vs. 0.10 outside). [snippet] — [RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/ufajxx/v63y2007i6p56-70.html)
- Hoberg and Phillips' text-based network industry classification (TNIC), built from 10-K product descriptions, gives each firm its own peer set; text-based peers outperform traditional industry peers in explaining valuations and stock-market comovement, and comovement with text-based peers is strongest for systematic shocks. [snippet] — [NBER WP 15991](https://www.nber.org/papers/w15991.pdf)
- TNIC is reported as roughly 25-40% more informative than SIC codes in explaining cross-sectional firm characteristics, and shocks to TNIC peers that are *not* SIC peers generate large momentum effects (i.e. links that fixed classifications miss are slow to be priced). [snippet] — [Hoberg and Phillips, JFQA, "Text-Based Industry Momentum"](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/6E04891A7AD30C24C21CBDE37EC06BF4/S0022109018000479a.pdf/textbased_industry_momentum.pdf)
- A 2026 Wharton working paper reports that firms whose GICS peers converge with them show higher TNIC product similarity and stronger return comovement. Preliminary. [snippet] — [Wharton accounting working paper, April 2026](https://accounting.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/OS-April-2026.pdf)
- Vendor models avoid forcing one label per firm: USE4 uses a multi-industry scheme mapping a firm's business activities to several GICS industries, and Bloomberg uses industry betas rather than 0/1 dummies. — [MSCI USE4 factsheet](https://www.msci.com/documents/10199/242721/Barra_US_Equity_Model_USE4.pdf) [primary]; [Bloomberg MAC3](https://www.bloomberg.com/professional/products/risk/mac3) [snippet]
- Statistical (PCA-type) risk models exist as a vendor product precisely to capture common drivers that fundamental labels miss. [snippet] — [Axioma/SimCorp statistical models paper](https://view.ceros.com/simcorp/axwp-statistical-models-r2-pdf)

### Inferences
- With ~90 large caps, a GICS sector/industry label per ticker is static reference data that can be hand-entered once (labels are printed on any quote page); no vendor feed is needed. The evidence says this beats pure correlation clustering for large caps, so labels should be the base and correlation the supplement.
- With only ~90 names, 6-digit GICS groups will often have one to three members, too few for a stable peer return. A practical compromise is hand-curated groups of about 4-10 names that mix GICS with thematic links (e.g. "AI/semis/hyperscaler capex"), plus a fallback to the 11-sector level. Sector ETFs' daily prices are a free alternative peer series that avoids the small-group problem; this is my suggestion, not a sourced practice.
- Always compute the peer return leaving the stock itself out, otherwise a single-stock crash partly "explains itself", worst in small groups.
- Rolling residual-correlation clustering is useful as a **diagnostic**, not as the definition: when two stocks' market-residuals have been highly correlated for 60-120 days but they sit in different hand-made groups, a theme is linking them (the Hoberg-Phillips result that non-classification peers matter supports this).
- **"The peer group is the story" pitfall:** if the whole AI/semiconductor group falls 6% and stock X falls 6.5%, the residual is small and a naive gate says "nothing stock-specific". That is correct as attribution but wrong as a reason to do nothing: the group-level move needs one investigation, shared by all members, and the answer may bear on each member's thesis (demand, capex, regulation). So attribution should route work to the right *level* (market / group / stock), not merely suppress it.
- Second pitfall: the leader is the group. When the group's move is caused by one member's news (bellwether earnings), leave-one-out peer returns for the *other* members look like a group event, while for the bellwether the same day is idiosyncratic. Detectable from asymmetry: one name with a much larger move and an earnings/news date.
- Third pitfall: conglomerates and platform companies (multi-industry firms) fit no single group; vendors handle this with fractional industry exposures. In the minimal version, estimate the peer loading gamma_i by regression instead of assuming 1, and accept a low-R-squared peer layer for such names.

### Gaps
- No source directly tests peer-group definitions for the specific task "decide whether a daily drop is stock-specific" at a universe of about 90 names.
- TNIC data is published free by Hoberg and Phillips for research use, per general knowledge; availability, update lag and licence terms were **not checked** in this session.
- Chan et al. numbers come from search excerpts and should be verified against the LSV-hosted PDF before being quoted.
- No research was done here on sector-ETF-based peer definitions or on correlation-clustering methods (hierarchical clustering, minimum spanning trees).

## 6. Published guidance on thresholds: when is a stock-specific residual large enough to warrant a fundamental re-check?

### Takeaway
I found no published institutional threshold of the form "re-check fundamentals when the idiosyncratic move exceeds X". The only documented numeric convention is statistical (the 5% significance level in event studies), and the literature on single-firm tests says that convention is both over-sensitive in volatile markets and under-powered in general. Any threshold for the bot is therefore a design choice to be calibrated on its own data.

### Cited Findings
- The only widely documented numeric standard is the 95% confidence convention for abnormal returns. — [Fisch, Gelbach and Klick, CLS Blue Sky Blog](https://clsbluesky.law.columbia.edu/2016/09/12/toward-a-better-understanding-of-event-studies-in-securities-litigation/)
- That convention is known to misfire in single-firm use: too many false positives when market volatility rises (understated standard errors) — [Baker, Stanford Law Review 2016](https://www.stanfordlawreview.org/print/article/single-firm-event-studies-securities-fraud-and-financial-crisis-problems-inference/) [snippet]; and a very high frequency of false negatives because single-firm tests have low power — [CLS Blue Sky Blog](https://clsbluesky.law.columbia.edu/2016/09/12/toward-a-better-understanding-of-event-studies-in-securities-litigation/) [primary]
- Using empirical quantiles of the stock's own past excess returns (SQ test) is the proposed replacement for normal-theory critical values. [snippet] — [Gelbach, Helland and Klick 2013](https://www.rand.org/pubs/external_publications/EP68377.html)
- Vendor risk systems scale volatility to the current regime rather than using a fixed historical sigma (USE4 Volatility Regime Adjustment). [primary] — [MSCI USE4 factsheet](https://www.msci.com/documents/10199/242721/Barra_US_Equity_Model_USE4.pdf)
- Pod-shop thresholds in circulation are drawdown limits on a whole book (about 5% soft, 7.5-10% hard), not residual thresholds on a single stock, and are unsourced. [lore] — [Quant Enthusiasts Substack](https://youngandcalculated.substack.com/p/risk-management-inside-a-pod-how); [Risk Hub](https://riskhub.org/blogs/multi-strategy-hedge-funds)
- Retail-level guidance argues the opposite direction: reviews should be triggered by events (earnings, management change, valuation threshold) and not by daily price moves. Not an institutional source. — [Kubera](https://www.kubera.com/blog/investment-thesis)

### Inferences
- **Proposed gating logic for the bot (my synthesis, not documented practice).** Compute once per day, before any per-stock AI call:
  1. *Market level:* SPY return in units of its own regime-scaled sigma, plus breadth (share of the ~90 names down, median beta-adjusted residual).
  2. *Group level:* each peer group's market-net return in sigma units, computed leave-one-out.
  3. *Stock level:* each stock's residual after market and group, as an empirical-quantile rank against its own past residuals, scaled for the current volatility regime.
  Then route: a large market term → **one** shared market research task whose output is attached to every affected stock; a large group term → **one** task per group; a large stock residual → the per-stock fundamental re-check. A stock can qualify at more than one level.
- **Choosing the stock-level threshold:** calibrate to a review budget rather than to a sigma rule. From the arithmetic in section 4, a one-sided 1% empirical quantile on 90 stocks yields roughly one flag per trading day in quiet conditions; a 0.5% quantile roughly one every two days. Backtest on the bot's own history to see the actual flag rate, especially on known broad sell-off days, where the rate should *not* spike if the market and group layers are doing their job. That non-spike is a direct test of the design.
- **Add a cumulative trigger:** the same residual logic over 5/20/60 trading days (CAR versus sigma x sqrt(n)) catches slow relative bleed, which for a long-horizon holder is arguably more thesis-relevant than a one-day gap.
- **Keep event triggers independent of price triggers:** earnings dates, guidance changes and similar events warrant a check regardless of residual size (consistent with the low-power finding: a small or absent price reaction does not show that nothing happened).
- **Do not treat "not idiosyncratic" as "no action":** see the peer-group-is-the-story pitfall in section 5. The attribution decides *which level* gets researched and how many times (once per level, not once per stock).
- Residual size says how surprising the move is, not whether the thesis is damaged; it is a trigger for investigation, never by itself a sell signal. This matches how the event-study literature treats abnormal returns (evidence that information arrived, not a valuation verdict).

### Gaps
- No institutional document, vendor note or CFA curriculum passage was found that prescribes a residual-size threshold for fundamental re-underwriting. This absence is itself a finding: thresholds appear to be firm-specific and unpublished.
- No published guidance was found on thresholds expressed relative to a peer group (e.g. "X% underperformance vs. sector over N days").
- Whether regime-scaling should use VIX, realized SPY volatility, or cross-sectional dispersion of the 90 names is untested here; Baker uses VIX, Barra uses its own cross-sectional bias statistic (details not read).
- All flag-rate figures in the Inferences are back-of-envelope and assume independence across stocks, which does not hold on sell-off days; they need a backtest on the bot's own price history.
